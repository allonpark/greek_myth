EPISODE 38 · ILIAD BOOKS 18–22

The Return of Achilles — A New Shield, an Angry River, and Hector's Death

Thetis · Shield of Hephaestus · Scamander · Hector · Athena · abuse of the corpse

Patroclus' death narrows the two fates of Episode 36 into something close to one. Achilles now knows that if he kills Hector, his own death will follow soon. Thetis tells him so again, and he does not withdraw. [A] The choice of a short life at Troy is no longer an abstract heroic proposition. It is made beside the body of his friend. The man who left battle over honor returns because of grief.

He has no armor. Hector is wearing the old armor stripped from Patroclus. Thetis therefore asks Hephaestus for new equipment, and Iliad 18 lingers on the shield. [A] It contains two cities, marriage and judgment, siege and ambush, ploughing, harvest, vineyard, herding, dance, and Ocean around the world. [A] A man returning to slaughter carries a surface crowded with life outside battle.

The shield cannot be treated as an exact technical drawing of a Bronze Age artifact. [D] Its imagery is poetic and may contain layers from different material worlds. [D] What matters inside the poem is the object’s narrative pressure: Achilles carries scenes of ordinary human life on the very shield that will accompany him back into killing.

In Book 19 Agamemnon and Achilles formally reconcile, and Briseis is returned. [A] Yet unlike the failed embassy, this settlement is not enough to explain Achilles' return; Patroclus is already dead. Briseis mourns him and speaks of the husband, brothers, and city she lost. [A] While elite men declare honor repaired, another cost of war remains physically beside them.

When Achilles returns to battle, the mortal-divine boundary bends differently from Episode 33. He pursues Apollo only to confront the fact that he cannot kill an immortal; then he chokes the river Scamander with bodies until the river god rises against him. [A] The river nearly overwhelms him before Hephaestus' fire intervenes. [A] A question first opened with Prometheus and Diomedes now appears as human violence provoking the landscape's divine personhood.

Hector remains alone outside the Scaean gates. Priam and Hecuba beg him to come inside. [A] It is the moment Andromache feared in Episode 35. Hector is conscious of shame and of earlier tactical failure, yet when Achilles approaches he runs. They circle the walls three times. [A] Courage and terror occupy the same man.

Athena then takes the form of Deiphobus and promises to stand with Hector. [A] He stops, fights, throws his spear, and only after asking his supposed brother for another weapon discovers that no one is there. He understands that a god has deceived him. [A] As with Pandarus in Episode 32, divine intervention and human action overlap rather than cancel each other.

Achilles' spear enters Hector's neck. Dying, Hector begs that his body be returned for burial; Achilles refuses. [A] The burial problem from Antigone in Episode 14 has moved to the Trojan plain. Does victory make the enemy body property, or does the dead person retain claims that war cannot erase? The poem postpones the answer.

Achilles pierces Hector's ankles, ties the corpse behind his chariot, and drags it away. [A] Priam and Hecuba see from the walls; Andromache learns the news later. [A] The horsehair helmet that frightened Astyanax and made his parents laugh in Episode 35 now sits behind an entirely different memory. Homer showed the family before the death, so Hector's defeat cannot remain only a battlefield result.

The Iliad still does not end. Hector is dead but unburied, and Achilles' rage has not fully dissolved. Even after Patroclus' funeral and funeral games, Hector's body remains in the Achaean camp. [A] Antigone once broke a king's command for a dead brother. Now an old king must enter the killer's hut to recover his son.

Reference & Evidence Notes

[A] Homer, Iliad 18–22. Direct source for the new armor and shield, Achilles' return, Scamander, Hector's pursuit and death, and abuse of the corpse.

[A] Iliad 22. Athena's disguise as Deiphobus, Hector's request for burial, and Achilles' refusal.

[A/D] Shield of Achilles. The description is primary text [A]; treating it as an exact reconstruction of a Bronze Age shield is modern interpretation [D].

[A: artifact] Louvre G153. Attic red-figure cup, c. 490–480 BCE, showing Achilles with Hector's corpse; Wikimedia Commons photograph released to the public domain.
