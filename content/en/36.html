EPISODE 36 · ILIAD BOOK 9

Can Honor Be Priced? — The Embassy to Achilles and His Two Fates

Iliad 9 · Agamemnon's compensation · Odysseus, Phoenix, Ajax · Briseis · kleos and nostos

After Hector leaves Andromache and their child and returns through the gates, the pressure of the war begins to move toward the Achaean ships. With Achilles absent from battle, the Trojans gain ground, and Agamemnon is finally forced to confront the fact that the fracture he created in Episode 30 has become a danger to the entire army. At the night council he lists the compensation he will offer Achilles: tripods untouched by fire, gold, horses, women, future cities, marriage with one of his daughters, and above all the return of Briseis. [A] The series has seen gifts before—Pandora, the golden apple at Thetis' wedding, the armor exchanged by Glaucus and Diomedes—but here the object changes function. Can a gift that created a relationship and a gift meant to repair one be understood in the same way?

The embassy consists of Odysseus, Phoenix, and great Ajax. [A] Remembering Thersites in Episode 31 changes the social shape of this scene. Not everyone in the army is equally entitled to speak to Agamemnon or negotiate with Achilles. Men of rank, long relationship, and recognized standing are chosen. Odysseus presents Agamemnon's offer with precision and translates Achilles' anger into a crisis for the whole coalition. Yet the long inventory of gifts does not sound to Achilles like a simple question of whether the amount is large enough. [A/D]

Achilles' answer is one of the strangest moments in the Iliad. Property can be regained, he says, but once a man's life has passed out, it cannot be won back by raiding or purchase. He then reveals what Thetis told him: two fates carry him toward death. If he remains at Troy, his homecoming is lost but his fame will not perish; if he returns home, his great fame is lost but his life will be long. [A] Until this point heroic honor can feel like an unquestioned currency. Achilles places life itself on the other side of the scale.

The question bends back toward Heracles in Episode 10, but not neatly. Heracles has traditions of apotheosis after death; Achilles' present choice offers no such simple exit. [C/D] He can imagine avoiding an early death, yet the price is the disappearance of the heroic future in which his name endures. The Epic of Gilgamesh also confronts a mortal hero with the problem of death and lasting name, but there is no evidence that Iliad 9 directly borrows this particular scene. [A/D] The safer comparison is that different ancient traditions made mortality force heroes to decide what, if anything, could outlast the body.

Phoenix then reaches backward into Achilles' childhood and tells the story of Meleager, a warrior who withdrew in anger and returned only when the cost had become extreme. [A] The embedded story is not a transparent moral. Meleager and Achilles are not identical, and Phoenix's choice of that story is itself an act of persuasion. [D] He wants Achilles to see a possible future version of himself in another hero's past; Achilles does not fully accept the analogy.

Ajax is shorter and harsher. Achilles, he says, has allowed anger to outweigh the honor of his companions. [A] Still Achilles does not return. Reducing this refusal either to selfishness or to righteous resistance erases the pressure of the poem. Agamemnon did insult him, and compensation cannot make that event unhappen. Yet Achilles' withdrawal also leaves other Achaeans to die. [A/D] The Iliad refuses to balance the moral accounts for us.

Late Bronze Age Hittite, Egyptian, and Amarna diplomatic records show gifts, compensation, marriage, and oath functioning as real instruments for creating and repairing political relationships. [A] That does not make Agamemnon's list a copy of any particular treaty. [D] The useful comparison is narrower: objects can carry status and obligation as well as material value. Achilles' problem is therefore not merely “how much will make him fight?” but whether a damaged relationship can be converted back into property.

Briseis reappears in the embassy as something to be “returned.” Yet, as Episode 30 noted, Iliad 19 later allows Briseis herself to speak over Patroclus' body about the husband, brothers, and city she has lost. [A] The war is not only a contest in male honor from her point of view. No matter how long the compensation list becomes, it cannot represent the whole experience of the woman whose transfer triggered the quarrel.

The embassy returns unsuccessful. More precisely, Achilles does not declare permanent indifference to everything that may happen. He leaves a boundary: he will hold back until Hector reaches the huts and ships of the Myrmidons. [A] The refusal is therefore not a closed ending but a waiting condition. The person who will finally break that condition is neither Agamemnon nor Odysseus.

It is Patroclus. He will put on Achilles' armor, lead Achilles' men, and cause distant observers to read one man as another. [A] The recognition problems seeded earlier in the series return on a battlefield where mistaken identity can move armies. Once another man can become “Achilles” long enough to change the battle, identity ceases to be private.

Reference & Evidence Notes

[A] Homer, Iliad 9. Direct source for Agamemnon's compensation, the embassy, Phoenix's Meleager narrative, Ajax's appeal, Achilles' refusal, and the two fates.

[A] Iliad 9.410–416. Achilles directly contrasts imperishable fame at Troy with a long life after homecoming; Perseus Digital Library edition.

[A/D] Late Bronze Age diplomacy. Hittite, Egyptian, and Amarna evidence provides comparison for gifts and relationship-making, not proof of a direct source for Homer.

[A: artifact] Louvre G264. Attic red-figure cup, c. 480–470 BCE, depicting the embassy to Achilles; representative photograph released to the public domain via Wikimedia Commons.
