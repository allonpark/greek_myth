EPISODE 21

How Could a Single Black Sail Bring Down a King?

Ariadne’s competing endings, the fate of Aegeus, and what followed after Theseus became king

Inside the Labyrinth, Theseus faced the Minotaur. Outside it, he faced something more ordinary and in some ways more dangerous: memory, promises, and communication. In the previous episode he escaped Crete with the young Athenians through Ariadne’s help. Later traditions also place Ariadne aboard the returning ship. The heroic formula appears complete—monster defeated, captives rescued, hero returning with the princess who enabled his victory—but Greek myth rarely lets homecoming function as a clean ending. It begins calculating the price of victory precisely when the ship turns homeward.

Did Theseus Really Abandon Ariadne?

The version best known today says that Theseus leaves Ariadne on Naxos and that Dionysus later takes her as his consort. Plutarch and other later sources preserve several incompatible explanations. [C] In some, Theseus deliberately abandons her; in others divine intervention compels the separation; another strand sends a pregnant Ariadne ashore on Cyprus during a storm. [C] The famous “Ariadne abandoned on Naxos” scene therefore did not descend from one immutable original.

An older surviving text tells a stranger story. In Odyssey 11, Ariadne appears among the women of the dead. Theseus had tried to bring Minos’ daughter from Crete toward Athens, but did not accomplish his purpose; on Dia, Artemis ended her life because of Dionysus’ testimony or accusation. [A] The familiar later sequence—betrayal followed by a happier union with Dionysus—is not what this early passage says. The lesson is methodological: the most famous version is not necessarily the earliest surviving one.

Archaic vase painting nevertheless confirms that Ariadne’s association with Dionysus was already old. [A: artifact] The representative image for this episode is an Attic black-figure amphora in the Metropolitan Museum of Art, c. 530 BCE, whose imagery includes Dionysus leading Ariadne. The Met photograph is Open Access and CC0. Text and image can preserve the same mythic names while telling different stories; that mismatch is evidence of a living tradition.

The Monster-Slayer Fails at the Simplest Task

Later tradition says that before Theseus sailed for Crete, Aegeus and his son agreed on a visual signal. If the expedition returned safely, the ship would raise a bright sail; if it failed, the dark sail would remain. [C] Theseus survives yet fails to change the sail. Some sources describe forgetfulness; others connect the lapse with grief over Ariadne. [C] Watching from afar, Aegeus believes the expedition lost and dies, after which the familiar aetiology connects his name with the Aegean Sea. [C]

The scene reverses the recognition episode from the previous chapter. The sword and sandals beneath the rock at Troezen transmitted correct information: Aegeus recognized the young stranger as his son and stopped Medea’s poisoned cup. An object saved Theseus. Now a sail transmits false information. Theseus is alive, but the ship announces loss, and the father pays the price. [D]

In the Oedipus story, people acted on misunderstood prophecies and incomplete knowledge. Theseus needs no oracle: the catastrophe can be read as a communication failure. [D] Sails, beacons, messengers, and visible signs really did carry information across distance in antiquity, but there is no historical evidence for this specific black-sail incident. [D] Myth makes an unsettling point: a hero capable of defeating an extraordinary monster can still fail at an ordinary promise.

Theseus the King: Where Myth Meets Athenian Politics

After Aegeus, Theseus becomes king. In the later tradition preserved by Plutarch, he then performs the synoecism of Attica, bringing scattered communities and political functions under a common Athenian center. [C] Classical Athenians could therefore remember Theseus not merely as a monster-slayer but as an explanatory founder of their political community.

Archaeology requires more restraint. Mycenaean occupation and elite centers are attested on the Athenian Acropolis and across Attica, but no contemporary inscription proves that one historical king named Theseus unified Attica in one identifiable event. [A: archaeology / D] Political integration was probably a long process; later Athenians may have compressed that complicated past into the biography of one founder. [D]

This kind of compression is not uniquely Greek. Mesopotamian king lists arranged complicated city and dynastic histories into continuous sequences of kingship, while Egyptian royal ideology embedded the union of Upper and Lower Egypt into royal identity. [A/D] The Hebrew Bible likewise concentrates the formation of monarchy and tribal coalitions around figures such as Saul and David. [A/D] None of this proves that Theseus was derived from an Egyptian or Hebrew founder; no such transmission is demonstrated. [D] The useful comparison is structural: societies often turn slow political formation into stories about singular founders.

Why Does the Successful King Begin Taking Risks Again?

Had the tradition stopped here, Theseus could have remained a tidy civic founder. It did not. Greek storytelling attached further adventures to him, especially his friendship with Pirithous, king of the Lapiths. In Plutarch and later mythography the two test one another’s courage, become companions, and embark on increasingly dangerous ventures. [C] That friendship will eventually carry Theseus toward one of the darkest choices in his mythology.

Before that comes the Amazon tradition. Theseus brings an Amazon woman—called Antiope or Hippolyta in different accounts—to Athens, and an Amazon army subsequently attacks Attica. [C] Sources disagree over her name, whether the relationship began as abduction or love, and how it relates to Heracles’ Amazon expedition. Yet the Amazonomachy of Theseus became a major subject in classical Athenian art. [A: artifact] After the Persian Wars, Athenians could use Amazon battles as a visual language for conflict between civic order and threatening outsiders, though the exact political reading remains interpretation. [D]

Comparisons with female warriors outside Greece require caution. Burials of armed women in Scythian cultural contexts have encouraged comparison with Greek Amazon traditions. [A: archaeology / D] They do not prove that one identifiable Scythian group created the Amazon myth. Mesopotamian Inanna/Ishtar possesses martial power, but a war goddess is not the same phenomenon as a society of human female warriors, and no direct genealogy to the Greek Amazons is demonstrated. [A/D]

Tradition makes Hippolytus the son of Theseus and an Amazon woman. [C] Later, Theseus is tied to the Cretan royal family once more by marrying Phaedra, another daughter of Minos. [C] Ariadne, who made Theseus’ escape from Crete possible, was also a daughter of Minos. Myth does not erase an old relationship; it places its bloodline inside the next catastrophe.

“Hero” Never Meant Moral Perfection

Modern usage easily turns “hero” into “good protagonist.” The ancient Greek heros was not necessarily morally exemplary. Extraordinary lineage, memorable deeds, power, and posthumous cult could define a hero even when the associated stories were violent, impulsive, or destructive. [A/D] Heracles, Achilles, and Oedipus all demonstrate that heroic status and ethical perfection are different categories. Theseus cannot be understood from the Minotaur episode alone.

As a young man he punished violent criminals on the road, subdued the Marathonian bull, defeated the Minotaur, and saved Athenian youths. Yet Ariadne’s fate remains fractured across traditions, the failed sail signal leads to his father’s loss, and his decisions as an older king become increasingly troubling. Rather than reading this as one clean arc of moral corruption, it is safer to remember that traditions from different periods and communities accumulated around Athens’ premier hero. [D]

The next catastrophe will center not on a monster but on speech. Phaedra falls in love with her stepson Hippolytus. Hippolytus rejects Aphrodite and devotes himself to Artemis. In Euripides’ surviving Hippolytus, divine anger, human desire, silence, accusation, and a father’s curse converge into disaster. [A]

In Oedipus, son and father were destroyed by failed recognition. On Theseus’ return from Crete, a false signal destroys the father. In the next story the direction reverses: a father’s judgment destroys his son. And Theseus’ special relationship with Poseidon will return in a radically different form.

Defeating a monster can be finished in a single act. Being a king, husband, and father requires new judgments every day. Escaping the Labyrinth did not mean Theseus had learned how to navigate the labyrinth of his own life.
