EPISODE 16 · ARGONAUTS

Why Couldn’t the Heroes Sail Straight Home After Winning the Golden Fleece?

The Argo’s Return — Apsyrtus’s Blood, Circe’s Purification, the Sirens, and the Bronze Giant Talos

The Golden Fleece was finally aboard the Argo. If we freeze the story at the end of the previous episode, Jason’s mission looks complete: the young man Pelias had effectively sent to die had yoked the fire-breathing bulls, survived the earthborn warriors, and taken the treasure guarded by the sleepless dragon. Yet Book 4 of Apollonius Rhodius’ Argonautica opens not with a triumphant voyage home but with flight. King Aeetes knows he has been betrayed, and Medea is no longer merely a princess of Colchis; she is a fugitive escaping beside her father’s enemy. [A] The fleece itself changes meaning. On the outward voyage it was the object to be won. On the return it is evidence that summons pursuit. The thread planted in the previous chapter now tightens: the more Jason gains, the more Medea loses.

Stealing the treasure and surviving the return were different problems

The Colchian fleet follows. In Apollonius’ surviving version, the pursuit is led by Medea’s brother Apsyrtus. He is not a small child but an adult prince capable of commanding men and cutting off the Argo’s route. The two sides eventually negotiate: the fleece may remain with the Argonauts because Aeetes had promised it if Jason completed the trials, but Medea’s fate will be submitted to an outside judgment. [A] For Medea, that compromise is terrifying. She has abandoned family and homeland to save Jason, only to discover that she can still be treated as the negotiable object in a settlement.

Jason and Medea therefore choose a darker solution. In Book 4, Medea lures Apsyrtus to a private meeting and Jason springs from ambush and kills him with his sword. Medea turns away, yet her brother’s blood stains her clothing. [A] A famous later variant tells the story differently: Medea escapes with a younger brother, kills and dismembers him, and scatters the pieces so that Aeetes must stop to recover his son. [C] These traditions should not be fused. Apollonius gives us an adult commander and makes Jason the immediate killer. The horror persists across versions, but responsibility is distributed differently.

Most importantly, the killing is not treated as an ordinary military success. Zeus is angered by the bloodshed, and the Dodona oak beam built into the Argo speaks with a human voice: they will not escape endless wandering and storms unless the pollution of Apsyrtus’s murder is purified. [A] Here the concept of miasma returns from our discussion of Antigone. Killing in battle and spilling blood through violated kinship, oath, and negotiation were not necessarily imagined as morally or ritually identical acts. [D]

Circe is not merely another witch; she is Medea’s kin

The person who can cleanse them is Circe, the same figure who will later transform Odysseus’ companions in the Odyssey. Apollonius places Circe and Aeetes within the family of Helios, making Medea’s encounter with her a return into her own extended divine lineage. Circe initially performs purification without knowing the whole story: blood, offerings, and rites addressed to Zeus the Cleanser remove the ritual stain of homicide. [A] Once she learns what happened, however, she does not endorse the deed. Purification is not acquittal. Treating an ancient cleansing rite as the equivalent of a modern legal pardon would erase an important distinction. [D]

Blood carries similarly expansive meanings elsewhere in the ancient Near East. In Genesis, Abel’s blood cries from the ground after Cain kills him; homicide disrupts not only a relationship between two men but the relation between human beings, land, and God. [A] Mesopotamian ritual corpora likewise use water, sacrifice, incantation, and substitution to remove impurity or dangerous influence. [A/D] None of this proves that Greek miasma was borrowed directly from a specific Mesopotamian or Hebrew text. The safer comparison is structural: in several ancient societies, violent death could damage a network larger than the killer’s private conscience. [D]

The Sirens are defeated not by wax, but by a stronger song

Purification does not end the voyage’s dangers. The Argo soon approaches the Sirens. Here the contrast with the Odyssey is revealing. Odysseus plugs his crew’s ears with wax and has himself tied to the mast. The Argonauts possess a different technology: Orpheus. When the Sirens’ voices begin to pull the sailors toward shore, Orpheus strikes his lyre and overwhelms their song with his own music. [A] The ensemble logic of the Argo reappears. A danger that cannot be solved by Heracles’ strength is answered by a musician.

Even then, not everyone is immune. Butes leaps into the sea under the Sirens’ spell, though Aphrodite rescues him. [A] The Sirens are therefore difficult to reduce to ordinary monsters: their weapon is not claw or fang but desire that persuades the victim to approach voluntarily. Comparisons have been made with bird-women and dangerous female beings elsewhere in the eastern Mediterranean, but no surviving Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Hittite, or Hurrian text can simply be labeled the source of the Greek Sirens’ maritime song. [D]

The return route follows mythic memory more than a modern map

Trying to plot Book 4’s itinerary literally on a modern nautical chart quickly produces difficulties. The Argo moves through an enormous mythic geography linking the Adriatic, Italy, the Phaeacians, and routes extending toward Libya. Ancient poets were integrating local claims that “the Argo passed here” into a single return story. [A/D] The route is therefore safer to read as a map of accumulated Mediterranean memory than as a recoverable Bronze Age captain’s log.

Among the Phaeacians, King Alcinous must again decide Medea’s status when another Colchian force demands her return. His criterion is striking: if she is still unmarried, she should be returned to her father; if she is already Jason’s wife, he will not separate husband and wife. Queen Arete quietly warns the couple, and Jason and Medea arrange a hurried marriage. [A] What began as desire becomes legal and political protection. Marriage is not merely the romantic ending of the relationship; it becomes an institution that allows an exiled woman to continue toward Greece. [D]

Then a bronze man appears on the coast of Crete

Near the end of the return, the Argo attempts to land on Crete. A gigantic figure tears rocks from the cliff and hurls them toward the ship. His name is Talos. Apollonius describes him as the last survivor of a bronze race and as a guardian given by Zeus to Europa. He circles Crete three times a day. His bronze body is nearly invulnerable, but near the ankle a blood-red channel, covered by a thin membrane, carries the boundary between life and death. [A]

Modern readers understandably call Talos an “ancient robot.” He does resemble an autonomous metal guardian, and the analogy has made him a popular ancestor in histories of automata. Yet the ancient text does not classify him as a programmable machine built according to mechanical engineering. He overlaps the categories of bronze race, divine gift, living guardian, and manufactured body. “The first robot” is therefore a useful modern analogy [D], not an ancient technical description [A].

Once again Medea steps forward. In Apollonius she invokes death spirits and attacks Talos through hostile gaze, incantation, and terrifying phantoms. As he lifts stones, he grazes his vulnerable ankle on a sharp rock; ichor pours out like molten lead and the giant crashes down. [A] Later traditions can make the mechanism more mechanical, with a plug or nail removed from the ankle so that the life-fluid escapes. [C] Classical Attic vase painting also preserves Talos collapsing among Medea, the Dioscuri, and the Argonauts, confirming that the scene had a strong visual life as well as a literary one. [A: artifact]

Ancient West Asia offers useful but limited comparisons. Mesopotamian lamassu and Anatolian sphinx guardians protected gates, palaces, and political boundaries; Egyptian monumental guardians similarly linked power, sacred space, and colossal bodies. [A: archaeology] But none of these beings patrols an island three times daily or dies through a single vulnerable vein. A shared eastern Mediterranean imagination of the giant guardian may be discussed [D]; direct derivation of Talos from any one of those figures cannot presently be demonstrated.

The person transformed most was not the hero holding the fleece

When Medea left Colchis she was a young princess with knowledge of drugs, plants, and ritual. By the end of the return she has neutralized the dragon, participated in her brother’s death, undergone purification for bloodshed, survived pursuit and the Sirens, secured a marriage under political pressure, and brought down a bronze guardian. Jason has acquired the Golden Fleece. But if we measure transformation rather than possessions, Medea has traveled farther than anyone aboard the Argo. [D]

Now the prow finally points toward Iolcus. Pelias believed the task impossible, yet Jason is alive and the fleece is with him. Should the king simply surrender the throne? Myth refuses so easy an ending. We still have to learn what happened to Jason’s family during his absence, what “miracle” Medea will demonstrate to Pelias’s daughters, and why a victorious hero will eventually be forced away from his own homeland.

In the next episode the adventure story becomes a revenge story inside a city. At its center stands the woman whose name will become one of the most frightening in Greek tragedy: Medea. Until now she has repeatedly saved Jason. The next question is different: what, exactly, did Jason promise the person who saved him?

Reference & Evidence Notes

[A] Apollonius Rhodius, Argonautica Book 4 is the principal continuous source for adult Apsyrtus and his pursuit, Jason’s ambush, Zeus’s anger, Circe’s purification, Orpheus and the Sirens, the Phaeacian arbitration, and Medea’s defeat of Talos. Classical text / translation.

[C] Later mythographic traditions vary especially over Apsyrtus’s age and killer and the exact mechanism of Talos’s death. They are kept distinct here rather than blended into a single “canonical” version.

[D] Near Eastern guardian comparisons, ritual parallels, and the description of Talos as a “robot” are modern comparative interpretations, not claims of direct borrowing.

Visual evidence: Classical Attic pottery depicts the death of Talos with Medea and the Argonauts. The header image used here is René Boyvin after Leonard Thiry, Medea Embarks with Jason (1563), National Gallery of Art: a later reception image, not an ancient artifact, released CC0/Public Domain.
