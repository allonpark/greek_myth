EPISODE 23

Why Could the Hero Who Tried to Steal the Queen of the Underworld Not Rise from His Chair?

Theseus and Pirithous — the abduction of Helen, the Chair of Forgetfulness, Heracles’ rescue, and the fall of a hero

After the death of Hippolytus, Theseus can no longer be remembered only as the young hero who killed the Minotaur and rescued the Athenian youths. In the previous episode, a father’s premature judgment destroyed his most innocent son. Now Theseus descends still further because of a choice no one forces upon him. His companion is Pirithous, king of the Lapiths. Their friendship was deeply embedded in heroic tradition, and later mythographic accounts present them as comrades in the battle against the Centaurs and as friends bound by dangerous oaths. [A/C] Yet this is not a friendship that consistently makes either man wiser. At a certain point, each begins to magnify the other’s recklessness. [D]

How far can friendship oblige a hero to go?

The Epitome of Pseudo-Apollodorus says that the two men agreed that each would marry a daughter of Zeus. [C] Theseus chose Helen of Sparta. The woman who will later stand at the center of the Trojan War suddenly appears here, much earlier in her life, inside the cycle of Theseus. Apollodorus calls her twelve years old; Diodorus Siculus says ten. [A/C] The precise age varies, but the shared point is clear: this Helen is not yet Menelaus’ queen and not yet the woman who sails away with Paris. She is already a royal girl whose beauty makes her the object of abduction.

According to Diodorus, after the death of his wife Hippodameia, Pirithous visits Theseus. The two seize Helen, then cast lots to decide which man will have her, while the winner swears to help the other obtain another bride regardless of the danger. [A] Theseus wins the lot. He hides Helen at Aphidna and places his mother Aethra with her. The episode sits uneasily beside the heroic image built in the Minotaur story. The man who once freed young Athenians from coerced sacrifice now forcibly removes a young princess from another city. Greek heros did not mean the same thing as a modern moral exemplar, and this episode makes that distinction impossible to ignore. [D]

Archaic art shows that the abduction was not merely a late mythographer’s invention. An Attic red-figure amphora by Euthymides, around 510 BCE, depicts Theseus carrying off Helen with Pirithous present. [A: artifact] This proves that the story was already circulating in visual culture by the late sixth century BCE. It does not prove that a historical princess of Sparta was abducted by a historical Theseus. What archaeology and art can establish here is the antiquity of the tradition, not the historicity of the event. [D]

Why does Helen’s first abduction already point toward Troy?

While Theseus and Pirithous are away in the underworld, Helen’s brothers Castor and Polydeuces — the Dioscuri — act. In the traditions preserved by Apollodorus and Plutarch, they invade Attica, recover Helen, and take Theseus’ mother Aethra captive. [A/C] In Theseus’ absence, Menestheus gains control of Athens; in Apollodorus’ version, the Dioscuri themselves restore him to power. [C]

The pattern forms an eerie mirror of the later Trojan War. When Paris later takes Helen, Greek kings and heroes will cross the sea to bring her back. Here, in an earlier heroic generation, Theseus takes Helen first and her brothers come in force to recover her. [D] This is not evidence that the story is a historical prototype of the Trojan War. It is evidence that Greek tradition repeatedly positioned Helen at the center of abduction, dynastic honor, and armed recovery.

The capture of Aethra plants an even longer thread. Later tradition says that Aethra is eventually taken to Troy with Helen and, when the city falls, is recovered by Theseus’ sons Demophon and Acamas. [C] Thus an episode in the Theseus cycle reaches forward to the final days of a war that has not yet begun in our narrative. Heroic myth is less a line of isolated tales than a network of families whose unfinished consequences reappear generations later.

The second bride was not human

Now Theseus owes his friend the other half of their oath. Since Theseus has obtained a daughter of Zeus, Pirithous must have one as well. His choice, however, is a married woman whose husband is no mortal king. He wants Persephone, queen of the underworld and wife of Hades. Apollodorus and Diodorus both preserve the descent undertaken by the two heroes in order to obtain her. [A/C] Diodorus adds a significant nuance: Theseus initially tries to dissuade Pirithous from the plan because it is impious, but the oath binding them compels him to follow. [A]

This matters. In Apollodorus’ compressed account the pair can look equally reckless. Diodorus preserves a version in which Theseus understands the danger and still goes because loyalty to his friend and fidelity to an oath have become obligations of their own. [A] That does not remove responsibility. It reveals how seriously ancient narrative could treat sworn friendship. A virtue pursued without limit can collide with other sacred boundaries. [D]

Their mission is not simply a visit to the dead. Orpheus descends in order to retrieve Eurydice. Heracles enters Hades as part of his labor to bring Cerberus to the upper world. Theseus and Pirithous, by contrast, intend to remove the wife of the lord of the dead. [D] Persephone is not merely a woman residing underground; in the Demeter tradition she is queen of Hades, and her absence and return are tied to the order by which grain and human life continue. Treating her as a bride to be seized is not merely heroic daring. It is an assault on the boundary itself.

Why did Hades not draw a sword?

In the Epitome, Hades answers with remarkable calm. He does not immediately attack the intruders. Instead, pretending that they will first enjoy hospitality, he has them sit down. The seat is the Chair of Forgetfulness. Theseus and Pirithous become fixed to it and are held fast, in Apollodorus’ account, by serpent coils. [C] The heroes still possess all the courage and physical power that served them above ground, but none of it matters. Hades does not defeat their strength; he removes their ability to act.

The scene completes a pattern we have watched across the Theseus cycle. On the road to Athens, he answered violence with superior violence. In Crete he defeated the Minotaur through courage, strength, and Ariadne’s aid. The black sail exposed a failure of communication. Hippolytus exposed a failure of judgment. In Hades, Theseus is not even given a battlefield. [D] In the Labyrinth, Ariadne’s thread preserved the route back. In the underworld there is no thread. Forgetfulness itself becomes the trap.

Comparisons with other ancient underworld traditions sharpen the differences. In the Sumerian Descent of Inanna, the goddess passes through successive gates, relinquishes the emblems of her power, and reaches a death-like condition under the laws of the netherworld. Through Enki’s intervention she returns, but her return requires substitution. [A] The motif of a living divine being descending and returning can be compared with Greek katabasis, but Inanna’s story concerns divine death, power, and restoration, not two human heroes attempting to abduct the underworld ruler’s wife. No direct line of borrowing can be demonstrated from this similarity alone. [D]

Egyptian funerary literature is different again. The modern category called the Book of the Dead consists of spells intended to help the deceased pass through the dangers, gates, judgments, and transformations of the afterlife. [A] As the British Museum emphasizes, the Egyptian afterlife was imagined as a hazardous journey, but the traveler is fundamentally a dead person seeking successful continued existence, not a living hero making a raid into the kingdom of the dead. Spatial imagination can be compared; narrative function should not be collapsed. [D]

Numbers 16 in the Hebrew Bible provides another useful contrast: Korah and his company are described as going down alive into Sheol when the earth opens and swallows them. [A] Here too the living descend to the realm below, but this is divine judgment, not exploration or rescue. One may compare the notion that crossing downward can mark transgression and punishment, but there is no evidence that the story of Theseus and Pirithous derives from this passage. [D] Hittite and Hurrian religion likewise knew powerful chthonic deities and ritual relations with the world below, yet no surviving narrative closely matches two mortal heroes descending specifically to steal the underworld ruler’s spouse. [D]

Why did Heracles bring back only one man?

Theseus receives another chance only because a different hero descends. One of Heracles’ final labors is to bring Cerberus from Hades, and Apollodorus says that near the gates he finds Theseus and Pirithous. [C] They stretch out their hands toward him as though hoping to rise from death itself. Heracles takes Theseus by the hand and raises him. But when he attempts to pull up Pirithous, the earth shakes, and he lets go. [C]

This is the scene chosen as the representative image for this episode. An Attic red-figure calyx-krater of about 450–440 BCE depicts Heracles, Theseus, Pirithous, and Hermes together in Hades. [A: artifact] Now in the Metropolitan Museum of Art as 08.258.21, the vase provides direct evidence that the captivity of Theseus and Pirithous and the descent of Heracles formed part of Classical Athenian visual culture. The photograph is published on Wikimedia Commons under CC BY 2.5.

Why is Theseus rescued while Pirithous remains? The surviving sources do not give us a single theological statute with a precise penalty scale. Diodorus stresses that Pirithous was the direct suitor of Persephone, while other versions even say that neither man returned. [A/C] It would therefore be too neat to claim that Theseus’ sentence was reduced by a known legal principle. [D] Narratively, however, the distinction is powerful: the man who follows his friend’s impious plan barely escapes; the man at the center of the plan remains below.

Plutarch preserves an even more striking rationalizing version. Some ancient writers transformed the underworld adventure into an allegedly historical episode involving a Molossian king named Aidoneus, his wife Persephone, and a savage dog named Cerberus. Theseus and Pirithous come to seize the king’s daughter, are caught, Pirithous dies, and Theseus is eventually released through Heracles’ intervention. [C/D] This does not reveal the ‘real historical event’ behind the myth. It reveals that ancient authors themselves were already trying to reinterpret myth in rational, quasi-historical terms.

He returned alive, but there was no kingdom waiting for him

When Theseus comes back to the world of the living, the cruelest fact is that the world has not waited. Helen has been recovered by the Dioscuri, Aethra has been carried away, and political power in Athens has passed to Menestheus. [C] In Apollodorus, Theseus returns but is driven out, goes to Scyros, and is eventually killed when King Lycomedes throws him from a height. [C] The details vary across traditions, but the end contrasts sharply with the victories of his youth: the slayer of the Minotaur dies isolated and politically defeated.

This may be the deepest reversal in the Theseus cycle. He is the man who escaped the Labyrinth, yet his life repeatedly opens into larger labyrinths: Ariadne, the black sail, Hippolytus, Helen, and finally Persephone. His failures are rarely failures of strength. Most of the time he is strong enough. The harder distinction is between what a hero can do and what he should do. [D]

And we should remember Helen. The girl abducted by Theseus returns to Sparta. In later tradition, time passes, suitors gather, Menelaus becomes her husband, and an oath among Greek princes eventually becomes one of the mechanisms that mobilize the expedition against Troy. [C] The walls of Troy are still distant, but the narrative’s gravity is beginning to pull in that direction.

We should not rush there yet. One major figure of the preceding heroic generation still links the Argonautic world, the Calydonian Boar Hunt, and later ideas about women, athletic competition, and marriage.
