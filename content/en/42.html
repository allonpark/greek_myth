EPISODE 42 · AENEAS & ROME

How Did a Survivor of Troy Become an Ancestor of Rome? — Aeneas, Dido, the Underworld, and Latium

Homeric Aeneas · Virgil's Aeneid · Anchises · Dido · Carthage · underworld · Latium · Alba Longa · Roman foundation memory

Aeneas survives the fall of Troy in Episode 40, but the first task now is to resist turning “Aeneas” into one fixed story. In the Iliad he is a Trojan fighter and son of Aphrodite, and Poseidon says that his line is destined to survive. [A] Dido and Carthage, the Golden Bough, the underworld vision of Rome, and the great war in Latium belong centrally to Virgil's Aeneid, composed in the first century BCE. [C]

The flight from Troy itself, however, is not a Roman invention. By the late sixth century BCE Greek vase painting already shows Aeneas carrying his aged father Anchises away from Troy. [A: artifact] A black-figure amphora at the Metropolitan Museum, around 500 BCE, preserves exactly this image. Aeneas as Trojan survivor is therefore much older than Virgil; the specific road by which that survivor becomes an ancestor of Rome belongs to a long later development.

In Aeneid 2, Aeneas leaves burning Troy carrying Anchises and leading his son Ascanius. His wife Creusa is lost in the escape; her shade tells him that another land awaits. [C] Andromache in Episode 35 feared the destruction of a family before it happened. Here survival itself is partial: a family leaves the city, but not intact.

This can be compared loosely with survivors after the floods of Episode 8. Deucalion and Atrahasis also stand beyond catastrophe and before a new order, but there is no evidence of direct borrowing from those flood stories into Aeneas' Roman destiny. [D] The difference matters: flood narratives often refound humanity or a community after cosmic disaster, whereas Aeneas becomes the carrier of political genealogy after military defeat.

Dido especially requires chronological caution. The conventional foundation date of Carthage lies in the late ninth century BCE, centuries after traditional dates assigned to the Trojan War. [D: chronological problem] Virgil compresses that distance and brings Aeneas and Dido together. [C] Dido receives the refugees; love, rulership, and obligation become entangled; divine command and Aeneas' departure drive the relationship toward catastrophe. [C] This cannot be read as a historical meeting between Bronze Age Troy and historical Carthage.

For Roman readers, however, Dido could powerfully prefigure later hostility between Rome and Carthage. [D] Helen in Episode 26 was made to carry arguments about a woman's movement and the causes of war; Dido, by contrast, is abandoned in the course of a male hero's movement. They do not belong in one moral category, yet heroic journeys repeatedly leave costs in women's lives.

The underworld thread from Episode 23 expands dramatically in Aeneid 6. Guided by the Sibyl, Aeneas descends and meets Anchises, who shows him figures of Rome's future. [C] Odysseus' katabasis gave knowledge needed for homecoming and memory of the dead. Aeneas' katabasis turns the underworld into a theater of a political future not yet born. The shared motif performs a different narrative job.

Arrival in Latium does not make Aeneas a peaceful founder. The story moves through Latinus, Lavinia, Turnus, and war. [C] The Aeneid itself ends not with the founding of Rome but with Aeneas killing Turnus. [C] A future city may be promised, but the poem's last surviving image is not tidy reconciliation. That matters for the final episode's question about how cleanly foundation myth can explain historical beginnings.

After Aeneas, traditions of Alba Longa, Romulus and Remus, and Rome's foundation develop across Roman writers. [C] Chronologies and genealogies are adjusted, and king lists bridge the large interval between Troy and Rome. [C/D] It is more useful to see this as a way communities connect political identity to an older heroic past than to treat the resulting chain as direct Bronze Age documentation.

At this point the Chaos of Episode 1 returns from very far away. At the beginning, genealogy imposed structure on an uncertain first world. Now Roman memory orders a past beginning with Troy's ruins and Aeneas' survival. But divine genealogy and historical chronology are not the same kind of evidence. The final episode therefore stops extending the story and separates the layers we have accumulated: text, artifact, archaeology, memory, and reconstruction.

Reference & Evidence Notes

[A] Homer, Iliad 20. Surviving Greek evidence for Aeneas' Trojan role, divine parentage, and the survival of his line.

[C] Virgil, Aeneid 1–12. Roman literary synthesis for Dido, the flight from Troy, the Cumaean underworld, war in Latium, and Turnus.

[C/D] Roman foundation traditions. Later accounts of Alba Longa, Romulus, and Remus must be distinguished from direct Bronze Age evidence.

[A: artifact] Met 56.171.26, c. 500 BCE. Attic black-figure neck-amphora showing Aeneas carrying Anchises from Troy, substantially earlier than Virgil; Met Open Access/Public Domain.
