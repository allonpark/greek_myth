SPECIAL 01 · SCREEN & RECEPTION

How Close Are the Trojans and Odysseus We See on Screen to Homer?

Helen of Troy (1956) · The Odyssey (1997) · Troy (2004) · O Brother, Where Art Thou? (2000) · Troy: Fall of a City (2018) · The Return (2024) · Christopher Nolan's The Odyssey (2026)

This special uses [R] for modern reception/adaptation. [R] is not an ancient evidence grade; it marks choices made by filmmakers—what they select, remove, compress, or enlarge. The regular grades remain unchanged: [A] surviving ancient text/artifact, [B] lost ancient work known through ancient summary or fragment, [C] later tradition, [D] modern scholarly interpretation.

After forty-three episodes, the first question to ask of a Trojan War film is no longer simply “is it accurate?” The Trojan War itself does not survive in one book. The Iliad covers a short period late in the war, from Achilles' wrath to Hector's funeral. [A] Helen's departure, Iphigenia, Troilus, Achilles' death, the Wooden Horse, and the sack must be reconstructed from lost epics known through summaries [B], surviving retrospection in the Odyssey [A], tragedy, and later tradition [C]. A director adapting “the Trojan War” therefore has to choose a tradition before the first shot is made.

Helen of Troy (1956) — choosing romance before war

Robert Wise's Helen of Troy turns the war into a mid-century Hollywood historical melodrama. As BFI notes, the film places Paris and Helen's love at the center and makes their escape the political pretext for a Greek assault already desired for other reasons. [R] Episode 26 showed that ancient Helen is not one stable character: the Cypria summary, Iliad 3, Herodotus' Egyptian version, and Euripides' Helen pull in different directions. [A/B] Wise narrows that instability into a romance question: not “which Helen is true?” but “why would these two people choose each other?” [R]

Troy (2004) — removing the gods enlarges human responsibility

Wolfgang Petersen's Troy is one of the most influential modern visualizations of the war. BFI identifies its decisive move as stripping away the gods. Aphrodite no longer rescues Paris and Apollo is no longer an active divine force in battle; ambition, fear, politics, and personal choice carry the consequences. [R] That is a major departure from Episode 32, where human action and divine intervention overlap rather than replace one another. [A]

The change strengthens Hector as a political and domestic figure. The conflict explored in Episode 35—husband and father versus defender of the city—becomes central to the film. [R] Achilles is similarly modernized. The film preserves his hunger for lasting fame but compresses the extraordinary two-fates speech of Iliad 9, discussed in Episode 36. [A/R] His death also draws on later popular “Achilles heel” imagery rather than on anything narrated in the Iliad, which does not contain his death at all; the lost Aethiopis summary instead involves Paris and Apollo. [B]

Troy: Fall of a City (2018) — putting the gods back on stage

The BBC/Netflix series moves in almost the opposite direction. Netflix describes it as a story of love, war, identity, revenge, and belonging triggered by Paris and Helen, while the gods remain active agents. [R] This restores the double causation explored throughout Episodes 25–32: Paris chooses and Aphrodite intervenes; Helen acts, yet divine pressure remains part of the world. Human responsibility and divine agency coexist rather than cancel one another. [A/R]

The longer serial form also gives the Trojan royal family time to exist as a family rather than a roster of warriors. Priam, Hecuba, Hector, Paris, and Cassandra accumulate relationships before catastrophe. [R] That extra duration strengthens the eventual emotional force of the Hector-Andromache material of Episode 35 and Priam's journey in Episode 39.

The Odyssey (1997) — rearranging Homer into a large popular adventure

Andrei Konchalovsky's four-hour television miniseries with Armand Assante deliberately gives modern viewers more Trojan War context before the homeward journey. In contemporary interviews, Konchalovsky explained that he inserted material from the Iliad so viewers would understand Odysseus' role in Troy and the Horse. [R] Homer's Odyssey itself begins in Ithaca with Telemachus and reveals much of Odysseus' wandering later through retrospective narration. [A] The miniseries straightens that entrance into a more accessible war-to-homecoming line.

Yet it does not remove the supernatural. Athena, Poseidon, the Cyclops, Circe, and Calypso remain part of the same reality. [R] The production therefore turns the poem into a broad adventure while emphasizing, as the filmmakers said at the time, the emotional, physical, and psychological price of war and wandering. That emphasis converges directly with Episode 41's distinction between arriving home and becoming at home again.

O Brother, Where Art Thou? (2000) — discarding the events and carrying the structure

The Coen brothers move the Odyssey into the American South of the 1930s. Ulysses Everett McGill, the one-eyed Big Dan Teague, and singing women near the river evoke Odyssean names and episodes without creating a one-to-one retelling. [R] What survives is the architecture: a displaced man, serial detours, deceptive identities, strange encounters, a wife and rivals, and an obsessive movement toward home.

That makes the film useful for understanding adaptation itself. A work can abandon Bronze Age costume, gods, and geography yet preserve what it regards as the narrative DNA of nostos. [R] The question shifts from “how many Homeric events remain?” to “what did the filmmakers believe made a story Odyssean?”

The Return (2024) — remove the monsters and leave the man who came back

Uberto Pasolini's The Return performs the opposite experiment. BFI describes it as a stripped-back adaptation of the second half of the Odyssey: no Sirens, Cyclops, or Circe, but an intense study of Odysseus, Penelope, and the psychological consequences of war. [R] Ralph Fiennes' Odysseus returns not as a triumphant adventure hero but as a physically and psychologically damaged survivor.

This intensifies Episode 41's recognition problem. Landing on Ithaca does not complete the homecoming. Telemachus, Eurycleia, and Penelope must each recognize Odysseus in different ways, and Penelope's bed test proves identity through shared knowledge rather than appearance alone. [A] Pasolini enlarges this domestic aftermath until the slaughter of the suitors becomes less a simple restoration of kingship than the disturbing survival of a warrior's capacity for violence. [R]

Christopher Nolan's The Odyssey (2026) — making the audience experience the scale of wandering

Christopher Nolan's The Odyssey is now a released 2026 film rather than a future project. BFI lists Matt Damon as Odysseus, Anne Hathaway as Penelope, with Tom Holland and Charlize Theron among the cast; the film runs 173 minutes and was the first feature shot entirely using IMAX 70mm cameras. BFI's production interview reports a 91-day shoot across six countries. [R]

The pairing of Nolan with Homer is especially interesting because the Odyssey is already nonlinear. It begins without Odysseus in Ithaca, then later allows him to narrate many earlier adventures to the Phaeacians. [A] Nolan's long-standing interest in fractured chronology therefore meets a structure already present in the ancient poem. [R] Instead of simplifying the poem's temporal dislocation as the 1997 miniseries often does, the film can use disorientation itself as part of the viewer's experience of wandering.

Its other defining choice is physical scale. Where The Return removes monsters to enlarge the homecoming's emotional aftermath, Nolan uses IMAX to restore sea, gods, monsters, war, and distance as bodily spectacle. [R] One adaptation asks what war leaves inside a person; the other asks how large the ordeal must feel before home becomes meaningful again. Yet both return to the same question raised in Episode 41: after twenty years, is the man who comes home still the man who left?

Instead of asking which film is “most faithful”

Ranking these works by fidelity alone hides what each adaptation is doing. Helen of Troy enlarges romance; Troy removes gods to intensify human responsibility; Troy: Fall of a City restores divine agency; the 1997 Odyssey reorganizes a complex poem into popular adventure; O Brother carries structure rather than events; The Return leaves only aftermath; Nolan restores ordeal at maximum cinematic scale. [R]

Modern film and television are therefore not rival answers to ancient texts. They are new layers of reception. Just as Greek tragedy altered inherited myth and Virgil reconfigured Aeneas for Rome, modern directors place their own period's concerns onto old stories. The 2004 Troy saw politics and human ambition; the 2024 The Return saw trauma; Nolan's 2026 Odyssey sees time, physical scale, and cinematic immersion. [R]

Episode 43 refused to treat the stones of Hisarlık and Homer's poetry as the same kind of evidence. The same discipline belongs here. Film is not evidence for the Bronze Age Trojan War. It is evidence for what later cultures want Achilles, Hector, Helen, and Odysseus to mean. One day Nolan's The Odyssey will itself be an old adaptation, and future audiences may watch it not only to encounter Homer but to understand what the early twenty-first century wanted an Odyssey to feel like.
