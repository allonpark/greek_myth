EPISODE 43 · FINALE

Where Does Myth End and History Begin? — Troy, Wilusa, Mycenaean Collapse, and the Boundary of Memory

Hisarlık · Troy VI/VIIa · Wilusa · Ahhiyawa · Hittite texts · Late Bronze Age collapse · oral tradition · Roman foundation memory · Evidence Grades

Episode 1 began with Chaos. The danger then was turning a gap too quickly into a finished order. The danger in Episode 43 is similar, except that the surviving material is now stone walls, clay tablets, poems, images, and later memories. Draw one line through all of them and it becomes easy either to say “the Trojan War happened exactly as Homer recorded it” or “myth is fiction and therefore historically irrelevant.” Neither sentence matches the evidence with enough precision.

Hisarlık contains not one city but many cities superimposed across time. As UNESCO summarizes, the site preserves millennia of occupation, fortification walls, gates, and evidence for a lower city, while documenting contact between Anatolia and the Aegean. [A: archaeology] Late Bronze Age Troy VI and VIIa are therefore central to discussion of possible historical backgrounds for Trojan-war traditions. [D] Yet no excavated layer comes labeled “Hector,” “Achilles,” or “Wooden Horse.”

We met the same boundary at Knossos in Episode 20. A real Bronze Age palace of great complexity can help us understand why later labyrinth traditions found such a setting compelling; it does not prove that the Minotaur lived in a particular room. Troy works the same way. Fortifications and destruction layers establish a material world in which conflict was possible. They do not authenticate every duel in the Iliad.

Hittite texts make the problem more interesting. Documents including the treaty between Muwatalli II and Alaksandu, ruler of Wilusa, attest a political entity called Wilusa in western Anatolia. [A: text] Identifying Wilusa with Greek (W)Ilios and Ahhiyawa with the Achaean/Mycenaean Greek world is strongly supported in modern scholarship, but it remains a historical-linguistic identification rather than a label supplied by the Homeric poems themselves. [D]

The resemblance between the name Alaksandu and Alexandros—the other name of Paris—has long attracted attention. [D] It is suggestive, but it does not prove that Paris was a historical king of Wilusa. Similarity of names can strengthen a context of contact; it cannot by itself establish identity. As with Typhon in Episode 5, resemblance begins an argument rather than finishing it.

Ahhiyawa documents also show diplomacy, conflict, and western Anatolian problems involving Hittite and Aegean powers. [A/D] That makes the broad geopolitical world of an Achaean force operating around an Anatolian city historically conceivable. It does not turn the Iliad's individual scenes into archival events. Between geopolitical possibility and epic specificity lies a large space occupied by oral tradition, poetic reshaping, and cultural memory.

Around the twelfth century BCE, multiple political systems across the eastern Mediterranean—including Mycenaean palace systems and the Hittite empire—underwent severe disruption. [A/D: synthesis of archaeology and texts] Destruction, population movement, changes in exchange networks, and the disappearance of administrative writing do not occur in exactly the same pattern everywhere. Modern scholarship therefore treats single-cause explanations of a uniform “collapse” with caution. [D] How much of these upheavals Trojan tradition preserves cannot be measured directly.

This is where oral tradition matters. The Iliad and Odyssey reached forms recognizably close to the surviving poems centuries after the Late Bronze Age world they evoke. [D] Oral poetry can preserve old names, objects, routes, and social memories while also absorbing language, values, and narrative invention from later performance contexts. [D] “Bronze Age memory survives” and “a Bronze Age event was transmitted unchanged” are not equivalent claims.

Episode 40 made that distinction visible. The Wooden Horse appears very early in archaic Greek visual culture on the Mykonos pithos. [A: artifact] That proves the motif was already known; it does not archaeologically confirm an actual wooden infiltration device. Aeneas carrying Anchises also appears in Greek art centuries before Virgil. [A: artifact] That does not mean those images already contain Dido and the Augustan future of Rome. Evidence speaks only as far as it can.

Roman foundation traditions present the same issue. Long genealogies from Aeneas through Alba Longa to Romulus bridge chronological gaps in later Roman storytelling and historiography. [C/D] Archaeology can study settlement and urbanization in early Latium, but it does not verify the individual event of twins being nursed by a wolf. “Rome emerged historically” and “the foundation legend occurred as told” are different statements.

Does that mean myth is false and history is true? That division loses much of what the series has been following. Myth was a serious ancient language for organizing religion, identity, kinship, cities, war, and death. [A/D] Historians and archaeologists do not accept mythic narrative as a transparent record, but they do ask why particular stories survived in particular forms. Conversely, discovering a site does not certify every story associated with it.

The [A], [B], [C], and [D] grades used throughout this series are therefore not a hierarchy of truth. [A] means direct attestation in surviving ancient text or artifact; [B], a lost work known through ancient summary or fragment; [C], later tradition; [D], modern interpretation or reconstruction. Keeping them distinct does not weaken the story. It reveals how a story changes across thousands of years.

At the end of the road from Chaos to Rome, what remains is not one final answer but a boundary. The stones of Hisarlık are real. Wilusa and Ahhiyawa are real names on ancient clay. Hector taking leave of Andromache is real as a surviving ancient poetic scene. But these three kinds of reality cannot be handled in the same way.

Perhaps the most exact thing we can do is refuse to erase that boundary: listen to stone as far as stone can speak, read the tablet as far as its text goes, and when the poem speaks ask what it remembers and what it makes. The spaces between them should not be filled merely because an ending feels more satisfying when every gap is closed.

Episode 1 began with Chaos. Episode 43 ends at another gap. The difference is that the gap is no longer simply ignorance. It is the space we have learned to leave open so that evidence and memory remain distinguishable.

Reference & Evidence Notes

[A: archaeology] Archaeological Site of Troy / Hisarlık. UNESCO World Heritage Centre summarizes the long occupation sequence, fortifications, lower city, and the site's importance for Anatolian-Aegean contact.

[A/D] Hittite texts: Wilusa and Ahhiyawa. Treaties and diplomatic texts from Hattusa are ancient primary evidence [A]. Wilusa≈Ilios/Troy and Ahhiyawa≈Achaean/Mycenaean Greece are strong modern historical-linguistic identifications [D].

[D] Oral-traditional Homer. The relationship between Homeric poetry and Bronze Age memory is a modern interdisciplinary research question and must be separated from proof of individual events.

[D] Late Bronze Age collapse. The transformation of Mycenaean, Hittite, and eastern Mediterranean systems is reconstructed from regional archaeological and textual evidence and should not be reduced to one universal cause.

[D] Roman foundation chronology. Aeneas–Alba Longa–Romulus genealogies belong to later Roman tradition and historiography and are not equivalent to the archaeology of early Rome.
