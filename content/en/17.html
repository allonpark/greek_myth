EPISODE 17 · JASON & MEDEA

Why Did the Woman Who Performed a Miracle Teach Princesses How to Kill Their King?

Pelias’ Cauldron — how Jason and Medea returned with the Golden Fleece only to become exiles again

When the Argo finally turned toward the coast of Iolcus, the expedition seemed finished. Jason had survived Colchis, where Pelias had effectively sent him to die, and the Golden Fleece was in his possession. His companions had kept him alive at sea; Medea’s knowledge had carried him past the fire-breathing bulls and the dragon. On the return voyage, as we saw in the previous chapter, the blood of Apsyrtus forced Jason and Medea to seek purification from Circe, and they still had to pass the Sirens and the bronze guardian Talos. Now only the promise remained to be honored. Yet in Greek myth, return rarely means restoration. Odysseus will later learn the same lesson: arriving home and recovering one’s home are not the same thing. Jason is about to learn it first.

The Fleece Came Home, but the Throne Was Not Waiting

In the later systematic account preserved in Apollodorus’ Library, Pelias does not simply surrender power after Jason and Medea return. Medea engineers his death. She convinces Pelias’ daughters that she can restore an old person to youth. To prove it, she cuts up an aged ram, places it in a cauldron with her drugs, and appears to produce a young lamb. The daughters believe what they have seen. Medea then persuades them that the same procedure can rejuvenate their father. They cut Pelias apart with their own hands and put him into the cauldron. This time no rejuvenated king emerges. **[C]** Apollodorus then says that Pelias’ son Acastus buried his father and expelled Jason and Medea from Iolcus.

Yet the episode cannot be dismissed as a late author’s invention. Attic pottery from the late sixth and early fifth centuries BCE already depicts Medea, the daughters of Pelias, and the ram emerging from the cauldron. **[A: artifact]** The continuous literary account available to us is later, but the core image of cauldron rejuvenation was firmly established in Greek visual culture before the Classical period. The representative image for this chapter is one such ancient Attic vase. It is a useful reminder that the date of our surviving literary text is not automatically the date when a story first existed.

Is “Magic” Enough to Describe Medea’s Knowledge?

Modern retellings routinely call Medea a witch. Ancient texts, however, place her powers in an overlapping field of herbs, drugs or pharmaka, incantation, ritual, and divine ancestry. **[A/D]** In the Argonautica, she prepares substances that protect Jason from fire and the bronze bulls, helps subdue the dragon, and becomes decisive again against Talos. At Iolcus, the same knowledge is transformed into a promise to reverse age. The decisive mechanism is not knowledge alone but the desire of those who trust it. Pelias’ daughters do not wish to murder their father. They wish to restore him. Medea turns filial devotion and the fear of death into the instruments of killing.

Behind the cauldron stands one of humanity’s oldest dreams. In the Mesopotamian Epic of Gilgamesh, Gilgamesh searches for escape from mortality after Enkidu’s death and eventually obtains a plant associated with renewed youth, only to lose it to a serpent. **[A]** There is no evidence that Medea’s cauldron derives directly from Gilgamesh’s plant. **[D]** But both narratives touch the same ancient desire: if death itself cannot be abolished, can time at least be reversed? Egyptian mortuary religion pursued continuity of body, name, ka, and ba through a very different system, while early Hebrew traditions use human lifespan and the tree of life to mark the boundary between mortals and the divine. **[A/D]** The answers differ, but the problem of life extended beyond its allotted limit recurs across the ancient world.

Medea’s rejuvenation creates a particularly cruel inversion. Pelias’ daughters try to extend life and instead terminate it. They take up the blade because they love their father. The structure recalls Oedipus: an action intended to prevent a feared outcome becomes the mechanism that produces it. **[D]** Again myth asks whether human beings fail because they know too little, or because they become dangerously certain that they know enough.

Is Jason a Hero, or a Man Whose Throne Depends on Medea’s Acts?

Jason’s responsibility must be handled carefully because the traditions do not all distribute guilt in the same way. Apollodorus describes Medea deceiving the daughters of Pelias and then records the joint expulsion of Medea and Jason. **[C]** Some vase paintings place Jason at the cauldron scene as well. **[A: artifact]** This does not prove that every ancient version imagined identical complicity. But the narrative shift is unmistakable. In Colchis, Jason survived through Medea’s abilities. At Iolcus, it is again Medea who removes his political enemy. The success of the expedition’s nominal hero increasingly rests on another person’s dangerous interventions.

The previous chapters established a pattern: Jason gains while Medea loses. When he won the Fleece, Jason gained fame while Medea lost father and homeland. During the escape he survived while Medea’s bond with her brother ended in blood. At Iolcus the same imbalance deepens. Medea crosses another irreversible line to resolve Jason’s dynastic problem. Yet Jason does not receive a throne. Both are expelled.

The tradition in which Acastus restores order after Pelias’ death reveals the political limit of revenge. **[C]** Removing a king does not automatically create legitimacy. Even the Golden Fleece, the visible proof of heroic achievement, cannot substitute for civic recognition and dynastic succession. Jason’s first problem was whether he could complete an impossible task. His new problem is whether completing it actually gives him the right to rule. **[D]** The skills that kill monsters and the skills that make a king are not identical.

They Went to Corinth — and Here the Traditions Split

In the familiar sequence represented by Apollodorus, Jason and Medea go to Corinth after their expulsion, live together there for roughly ten years, and then catastrophe begins when King Creon arranges for Jason to marry his daughter Glauce. **[C]** Combined with Euripides’ Medea of 431 BCE, this became the backbone of the version most readers know today. But older Corinthian traditions make the picture much less simple.

Pausanias, writing in the second century CE, preserves material attributed to the earlier poet Eumelus. In that account, Helios had given the Corinthian territory to Medea’s father Aeetes. Aeetes left for Colchis, and after later rulers died out, the Corinthians themselves summoned Medea from Iolcus and gave her the kingdom. Jason became king of Corinth **through Medea. [B]** Here Medea is not merely a powerless foreign woman following a Greek hero. She can be remembered as the figure carrying dynastic legitimacy in Corinth.

In the same tradition reported by Pausanias, Medea hides each newborn child in the sanctuary of Hera in the hope of making the child immortal. The attempt fails; Jason discovers what she has done and leaves despite her pleas. **[B]** This is decisively different from the famous ending of Euripides that we will reach next. Not every Greek audience from every period necessarily inherited a single story in which Medea deliberately killed her children to punish Jason.

This distinction matters. The more shocking a mythic scene becomes, the easier it is to assume that it was always part of the story. The deaths of Medea’s children existed in competing traditions, and Euripidean tragedy made one configuration overwhelmingly famous. **[B/A/D]** The next chapter will therefore separate what Euripides inherited from what his dramatic arrangement made dominant.

A Woman from Outside and the Boundary of the City

Medea’s Colchian origin is more than an address in Greek literature. The princess from the far eastern Black Sea could stand inside the Greek heroic world without ever becoming entirely Greek. **[D]** If the Argonautic expedition sent Greek heroes outward into foreign space, Medea’s return reverses the direction: a person from that outer world enters Greece with them. Her knowledge of drugs and ritual can be praised as wisdom when it saves heroes, yet become an object of fear once political relationships collapse.

There is no evidence that this structure derives directly from a particular Hittite, Hurrian, or Mesopotamian woman. Yet royal marriage really did cross linguistic and territorial borders in the ancient eastern Mediterranean, and Late Bronze Age diplomatic correspondence documents foreign princesses moving between courts as instruments of alliance. **[A: textual/archaeological; D]** Medea should not be reduced to the memory of one historical marriage, but the social experience of a woman leaving her natal court and entering another dynasty was not merely mythical.

Hebrew narratives likewise sometimes use foreign royal women to expose political and religious boundaries, while Egyptian-Hittite diplomatic evidence records the movement of princesses as an official instrument of interstate relations. **[A]** The meaningful comparison is not that “foreign women were dangerous.” It is that marriage could alter kinship, kingship, diplomatic status, and religious belonging at the same time. **[D]** Medea’s tragedy will show what happens when all those bonds fail at once.

Now It Is Jason’s Turn to Break a Promise

In Colchis, Medea betrayed her father for Jason. During the escape, she became entangled in her brother’s death. At Iolcus she engineered the death of Pelias and became an exile again. None of this means that her actions should be morally excused; ancient tradition itself makes them increasingly dangerous and destructive. But the narrative debt between the couple has become impossible to ignore. Jason’s survival, return, and possession of the Golden Fleece are difficult to imagine without Medea.

That is why Jason’s decision in Corinth to marry another princess cannot be reduced to a story of jealousy. Euripides’ Medea will enumerate what she did to save Jason, what she abandoned, and the oaths she believes bound them. **[A]** Jason, in turn, will argue that a new royal marriage is a rational political decision intended to improve his own position and the future of the children. **[A]** Both possess arguments. The tragedy begins because those arguments can no longer inhabit the same moral world.

The next chapter enters the most famous—and most frequently simplified—night in Medea’s story: the poisoned gifts, the death of Creon, the fate of the children, the chariot of the Sun, and above all one historical question. Was the story that Medea herself killed her children already fixed before Euripides?

The heroic expedition that began aboard the Argo has now narrowed from monsters and oceans to a single household in a city. What happens inside that house will be remembered longer than almost any feat performed for the Golden Fleece.

Reference & Evidence Notes

[A] Late sixth- and early fifth-century BCE Attic black- and red-figure pottery preserves the scene of Medea rejuvenating a ram before the daughters of Pelias. Perseus Art & Archaeology’s Harvard 1960.315 (c. 510–500 BCE) and related British Museum vessels are important material evidence.

[B] Pausanias 2.3.10–11 preserves a Corinthian tradition attributed to the earlier poet Eumelus: Medea receives Corinth through the Aeetes line and attempts to make her children immortal in Hera’s sanctuary. Because the earlier work is lost and survives through a later witness, it is classified [B].

[C] Apollodorus’ Library, Book 1, provides the systematic later account in which Medea demonstrates rejuvenation with a ram, deceives Pelias’ daughters, and is expelled from Iolcus with Jason after Pelias’ death.

[D] Comparisons with Gilgamesh’s rejuvenating plant, Near Eastern ideas of life extension, and diplomatic royal marriages are structural comparisons only. They do not establish a direct source or borrowing route for the Medea tradition.
