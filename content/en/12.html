EPISODE 12

Did the Sphinx Really Ask the World’s Most Famous Riddle?

Oedipus: how every attempt to escape prophecy helped fulfill it

In the previous episode, Bellerophon crossed into legend on the back of Pegasus and faced the Chimera. At the edge of that story we glimpsed another winged hybrid creature. In Egypt, such beings could embody kingship and protection. In Hittite Anatolia, sphinxes stood at monumental gateways. In Mesopotamia, colossal composite guardians watched over palaces and thresholds. But when this creature reaches Greek Thebes, its function changes completely. It does not simply guard a boundary, and it does not fight by brute force alone. It asks a question. That creature is the Sphinx, and the man who stands before it is Oedipus.

Most of us think we already know this story. “What walks on four legs in the morning, two at noon, and three in the evening?” Oedipus answers “a human being,” and Thebes is saved. Yet when the ancient evidence is arranged chronologically, the familiar version begins to fracture. The exact riddle we memorize does not appear in the earliest surviving account of Oedipus, nor is it recited in Sophocles’ Oedipus Tyrannus. [A/C] Even more strikingly, the earliest surviving Oedipus does not meet the same end as the hero of Sophocles’ tragedy.

The Oedipus myth did not begin as a single finished book. Centuries of poetry, performance, vase painting, lost epic, commentary, and later mythography layered new memories onto the same figure. So this episode is not only about what happened to Oedipus. It is also about how the Oedipus we think we know was constructed over time.

The earliest surviving Oedipus story does not show us a Sphinx

The earliest surviving literary layer in which Oedipus’ fate is clearly visible appears in Book 11 of Homer’s Odyssey. During his journey among the dead, Odysseus sees a woman named Epikaste, the figure later tradition usually calls Jocasta. Homer says that she unknowingly married her own son, and that Oedipus, likewise ignorant of his true parentage, became entangled in the death of his father. The gods eventually revealed the truth, and catastrophe followed. [A]

So far the outline sounds familiar. But one detail changes everything: after the revelation, Homer’s Oedipus continues to rule Thebes. [A] That is very different from the Sophoclean ending that dominates modern memory. Moreover, Homer’s brief account says nothing about a Sphinx and nothing about the famous riddle.

That does not prove that a Sphinx story did not exist in Homer’s world. Homer may have omitted material already known to his audience, or another song now lost may have carried that episode. [D] The narrower claim is the safer one: in the earliest surviving narrative, the central tragedy is not the riddle but the king who discovers too late who he truly is.

So where did the “four legs, two legs, three legs” riddle come from?

By the fifth century BCE, the situation is clearly different. Attic vase painting repeatedly shows Oedipus facing a female, winged Sphinx perched on a column, rock, or raised support. [A: iconographic evidence] Sophocles’ Oedipus Tyrannus also assumes that the audience knows Oedipus once defeated the “singing” monster and saved Thebes. [A]

Yet Sophocles never has the Sphinx recite the exact riddle familiar today. The more explicit ancient formulation asking for the creature that is four-footed, two-footed, and three-footed is more clearly preserved in later textual and scholastic traditions. [C] The smooth modern version linking morning, noon, and evening to infancy, adulthood, and old age is the product of a long process of retelling.

So it is inaccurate to say simply that “Sophocles wrote the famous Sphinx riddle.” A more precise statement is this: by Sophocles’ time, Oedipus’ victory over the Sphinx through intelligence was already well known, but Oedipus Tyrannus does not preserve the exact wording that modern readers usually quote.

The Egyptian and Hittite sphinxes did not ask riddles

When modern readers hear “Sphinx,” they often picture the Great Sphinx of Giza. That colossal human-headed lion predates the literary Oedipus tradition by many centuries and is normally understood within the royal symbolism of Egypt’s Old Kingdom. [A: archaeology] The name “sphinx” itself is Greek and was applied later to Egyptian monuments. There is no known ancient Egyptian tradition in which the Great Sphinx stops travelers and asks an Oedipus-style riddle.

Move north into Anatolia and another set of sphinxes appears. At Alaca Höyük, monumental sphinxes guarded a Hittite gateway in the later second millennium BCE. [A: archaeology] Elsewhere in Mesopotamia and the Neo-Assyrian world, hybrid guardians such as lamassu combined human, bovine or leonine, and winged features in the service of royal power and threshold protection. [A]

It would be difficult to argue that Greek sphinx imagery developed in complete isolation from this eastern Mediterranean visual vocabulary. Trade, artisan mobility, and cultural contact moved forms from Egypt, the Levant, and Anatolia into the Aegean from the Bronze Age into the Orientalizing period. [D] But a crucial distinction must be maintained: the possible transmission of an image does not prove the transmission of the Oedipus riddle story.

The evidence may instead point to transformation. A protective or liminal hybrid image moved between cultures and, in Greece, acquired a distinct narrative role: not merely guardian, but tester of human intelligence. [D] Cultures do not always copy stories whole. Sometimes they borrow a body and invent a new personality.

The child sent away to defeat a prophecy

Now return to the version Sophocles shaped most powerfully. A son is born to Laius, king of Thebes, and Jocasta, but an oracle hangs over the child and the father he may one day kill. The royal house tries to prevent that future. The infant is exposed after his ankles are fastened or injured and is sent away. [A]

But the child who is meant to die survives. Passed through the hands of shepherds, he reaches Corinth and grows up as the son of King Polybus and Queen Merope. The name Oedipus is often connected with the idea of a “swollen foot,” but it is safer to treat this as a powerful literary association rather than a completely certain historical etymology. [D]

As a young man, Oedipus hears a claim that he may not truly be Polybus’ son. He goes to Delphi, hoping to learn who his parents are. The oracle does not answer that question directly. Instead, it confronts him with a horrifying future involving his own family. [A]

Oedipus loves the parents who raised him. So he decides not to return to Corinth. If he stays far from Polybus and Merope, he thinks, the prophecy can be broken. He sets out to travel away from fate. And that decision becomes the road toward it.

At a place where three roads meet, he becomes involved in a violent conflict with an older traveler and his entourage. He then continues toward Thebes. [A] He does not know that the old man is Laius, king of Thebes, and his biological father. At Thebes, Oedipus solves the Sphinx’s challenge, saves the city, receives the throne, and marries the widowed queen Jocasta. [A/C]

By fleeing Corinth to avoid prophecy, he has entered the road where his father travels and the palace where his mother waits.

Stories in which trying to escape the future helps create it

This structure is not uniquely Greek. In the Egyptian Tale of the Doomed Prince, preserved in a New Kingdom manuscript, supernatural powers announce at the prince’s birth that he will die through a crocodile, a snake, or a dog. His father responds by isolating him in an attempt to keep fate away. Unfortunately, the end of the surviving papyrus is lost, so we do not know how the story finally resolved its prophecy. [A/B]

The Hebrew Bible’s Joseph cycle offers another useful structural comparison. Joseph’s brothers act to prevent the future implied by his dreams, yet their very attempt sends him to Egypt and helps create the circumstances in which they later stand before him. [A]

But resemblance is not proof of borrowing. It would go beyond the evidence to claim that Oedipus came directly from an Egyptian or biblical story. The paradox in which an attempt to evade the future becomes the mechanism of fulfillment is powerful enough to arise independently. [D] What matters is what each culture does with that structure.

A plague strikes Thebes, and the king becomes an investigator

Oedipus Tyrannus does not begin with the young hero meeting the Sphinx. Years have passed. Oedipus is now a respected king, and when crisis returns, the citizens turn again to the man who once saved them. Thebes is being destroyed by plague: crops fail, livestock suffer, and human fertility collapses. [A]

The answer from Delphi is that the death of Laius has never been properly cleansed. The unresolved crime has become miasma, a pollution that contaminates the city. [A] Oedipus vows to identify and punish the murderer.

From this point the play takes on the architecture of a detective story. [D] There is an old crime, a location, surviving witnesses, conflicting testimony, and fragments of evidence that seem unrelated until they begin to align. The king is the investigator. But the audience knows what the investigator does not: the past he is hunting is his own.

The blind seer Tiresias tells him that the truth he seeks is tied directly to himself. [A] Oedipus refuses to believe it and suspects conspiracy. The reaction is understandable. This is the man who solved the Sphinx. He has built his kingship on the conviction that difficult problems yield to intelligence. And that becomes his trap. Because he solved the Sphinx’s problem, he believes every problem must have an answer that he can force into the light.

Truth does not arrive all at once

Jocasta tries to calm him by describing the past. Laius, she says, had also received an oracle involving a son, but the child disappeared long ago, and Laius himself died where three roads met. [A] The phrase strikes Oedipus because he remembers another three-way junction.

Then a messenger arrives from Corinth with apparently good news: Polybus has died a natural death. Oedipus briefly relaxes. The man he believed was his father died without his involvement. Perhaps the oracle was wrong after all. But the messenger, trying to reassure him further, reveals something far more dangerous: Polybus was not his biological father. Oedipus had been received as an abandoned child from another shepherd. [A]

Jocasta understands first. She begs Oedipus to stop asking questions. He refuses. The final shepherd is brought in, the last living link between the infant cast out from the Theban royal house and the child later raised in Corinth. [A]

At that moment all the separate identities collapse into one. The abandoned prince. The Corinthian prince. The traveler at the crossroads. The solver of the Sphinx. The king of Thebes. They are all Oedipus. The truth he has been searching for in order to save the city was never outside him.

The Sphinx’s answer was “human” — but did Oedipus know the human closest to him?

The famous riddle now acquires a deeper irony. Its answer is humanity: the creature that crawls in infancy, walks upright in adulthood, and adds a staff in old age. Oedipus can solve a problem about the entire human life cycle. Yet the human being he understands least is himself.

This is not an interpretation Sophocles explicitly spells out inside the play. It emerges when the Sphinx tradition and the drama are read together. [D] But the irony helps explain why Oedipus is more than a simple story about inescapable fate. The greatest problem-solver in Thebes is the last person to solve the problem of his own identity. Knowledge makes him king; fuller knowledge destroys the world in which that kingship made sense.

The Oedipus we know is not identical to the oldest surviving Oedipus

In Sophocles, Jocasta dies by her own hand and Oedipus blinds himself. [A] But as we saw at the beginning, Homer’s earlier surviving account allows Oedipus to continue ruling Thebes after the truth is known. [A]

The lost Theban epic known as the Oedipodeia also seems to have preserved family traditions that differed from the later standard version. Ancient testimony suggests variants in which Euryganeia, not Jocasta, was the mother of Oedipus’ children. [B/C] There is no single ancient “final edition” of Greek mythology. The familiar family tree is a composite created by reading different periods together.

Thebes was a real city — that does not make Oedipus a historical king

The Boeotian city of Thebes was not imaginary. Excavation on and around the Cadmea has revealed Mycenaean palatial remains and Linear B material, demonstrating that Thebes was an important political center in the Late Bronze Age. [A: archaeology]

But the reality of Thebes does not prove the historical existence of Oedipus. Troy can be archaeological without every scene of the Iliad being a chronicle. In the same way, destruction layers at Bronze Age Thebes cannot simply be assigned to a particular heroic war from later legend. [D] Archaeology establishes the reality of the stage, not necessarily the historicity of every actor remembered upon it.

Oedipus falls, but the tragedy of Thebes is only beginning

The royal house does not end with Oedipus. His sons Eteocles and Polynices remain. Who will rule Thebes? What first looks like a dispute inside a family will soon become a war that consumes the city. One brother remains inside the walls. The other gathers allies and returns from outside.

Then, one day, seven champions advance toward the seven gates of Thebes.

For the Greeks, this war belonged to the heroic generation before Troy. And at this point an old thread returns. In the Atrahasis tradition, we saw divine concern with an overburdened human world eventually answered by catastrophe. In one Greek strand, a different instrument enters the story: war.

What is terrible in Oedipus is that the crisis does not end as one man's punishment. It continues through his sons' war and Antigone's decision, turning an unresolved past into obligations for the next generation. Related pressures will appear again in the house of Atreus and the Trojan cycle. These are not one genealogical tradition, but they repeatedly ask how far an older generation can narrow the choices of those who follow. [D]
