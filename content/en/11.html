EPISODE 11 · BELLEROPHON AND THE CHIMERA

Why Did the Rider of Pegasus Fall from the Sky?

When a hero who has defeated monsters reaches for the realm of the gods, success itself becomes the cause of his fall

In the previous episode, the mortal body of Heracles disappeared into fire while his divine aspect was received on Olympus. A being born among mortals ultimately crossed into the company of the gods. Yet the winged horse born long ago from Medusa’s severed neck had another human partner whose ending would be very different.

Bellerophon.

He too kills a monster, wins wars, marries a king’s daughter, and receives royal land. He acquires almost every form of heroic success. Yet at the end of his story Homer describes him as hated by all the gods, wandering alone across the Aleian plain, avoiding the paths of humankind. [A]

Why? The oldest surviving account does not fully explain. Later traditions fill the silence with one unforgettable answer: Bellerophon rode Pegasus too high. Beyond monsters and human kingdoms, he tried to reach Olympus itself.

The story becomes a test of the boundary between exceptional mortals and gods.

The oldest detailed story begins with a death letter

Our earliest substantial surviving account of Bellerophon appears inside Book 6 of the Iliad. Bellerophon himself is not present. The Trojan War warrior Glaucus recounts his ancestry and tells the story of a heroic forebear. [A] Even to the heroes at Troy, Bellerophon belongs to an older heroic past.

Bellerophon resides at the court of King Proetus. The king’s wife desires him; after being rejected, she tells her husband that Bellerophon attempted to violate her. Proetus is enraged but reluctant to kill a guest directly. Instead he sends Bellerophon to Lycia with a folded tablet or message for his father-in-law. Homer says it contains destructive or deadly signs. [A]

Bellerophon carries his own death order.

The passage has long attracted historians of writing because Homer’s “signs” raise questions about what sort of writing or symbolic notation the poet imagined. We should not turn the scene into straightforward proof of everyday letter-writing in the heroic age; the precise historical implications are debated. [D] Narratively, however, the device is clear: the king uses a message instead of a sword.

The Lycian king feeds him for nine days before reading the message

When Bellerophon reaches Lycia, the king does not immediately inspect the tablet and execute him. Homer says he welcomes the stranger for nine days, sacrificing nine oxen; only on the tenth does he ask for the message. [A]

Seen through the Greek institution of guest-friendship, xenia, the scene is suggestive. [D] A man who has been fed and honored at one’s table has become difficult to kill with one’s own hands. So the Lycian king adopts the same indirect logic as Proetus.

He does not execute Bellerophon.

He gives him a task designed to do the killing.

Destroy the Chimera.

A lion, goat, and serpent in one body

Homer describes the Chimera as a divine or monstrous being with lion in front, serpent behind, goat in the middle, breathing terrible fire. [A] Hesiod develops the composite form through a clearer genealogy and places Chimera among the monstrous descendants associated with Typhon and Echidna. [A]

Now earlier episodes begin connecting. Typhon, Echidna, Hydra, Cerberus, and Chimera form overlapping branches of a monster genealogy; depending on the tradition, the Sphinx and Nemean Lion are also drawn into related lines. Greek heroic myth can therefore look like the gradual clearing away of an old monstrous family by Zeus and mortal heroes. [D]

Yet Pegasus is missing from Homer’s brief battle scene

The modern image is automatic: Bellerophon flies on Pegasus while the fire-breathing Chimera attacks below. Yet the brief Iliad account of Bellerophon killing the monster does not explicitly describe Pegasus. [A] Hesiod, however, directly associates Pegasus and Bellerophon with the killing of Chimera. [A]

By the fifth century BCE, Pindar enriches the story further. Bellerophon cannot easily master Pegasus; Athena appears in a dream and provides a golden bridle with which the winged horse can be tamed. [A] Across time, the narrative gains scenes: Homer’s concise heroic tradition, Hesiod’s explicit Pegasus connection, Pindar’s divine bridle.

Myth does not begin as a finished franchise bible. It becomes thicker as generations of poets and artists fill its spaces.

Could the body of the monster have crossed the sea before the story did?

Around the eighth and seventh centuries BCE, Greek art undergoes a major transformation. Animal, plant, and composite-monster motifs from the Near East and Egypt proliferate in what modern art history calls the Orientalizing period. Sphinxes, griffins, sirens, and other hybrid creatures become common in Greek pottery and decoration. [A/D]

We cannot prove that Chimera was copied directly from one specific Near Eastern monster. But the visual vocabulary of composite creatures was clearly circulating through the eastern Mediterranean. Objects could travel before stories were written down: pottery, metalwork, ivory, textiles, and the craftsmen who made them all crossed cultural boundaries. [D]

A Greek artist might see an imported creature and adapt its features; a storyteller might then attach those features to a local monster, or an existing story might attract a newly fashionable visual form. We rarely possess enough evidence to reconstruct the exact direction. But cultural transmission was not restricted to translated books.

The Chimera lives in Anatolia, not mainland Greece

Bellerophon’s major adventures take place in Lycia, in southwestern Anatolia, now part of Turkey. In the Iliad, after killing Chimera he fights the Solymi and then the Amazons. [A] On a map, his heroic geography is oriented toward Anatolia and the eastern Aegean rather than mainland Greece.

The region also contains the natural flames known today as Yanartaş, where gas escaping from rock burns continuously. Ancient geographic traditions came to associate persistent fire in Lycia with Chimera. [C/D] The fit is irresistible: a landscape that seems to breathe flame and a monster famous for breathing fire.

But cause and association must be separated. The flames are real; the claim that they originally generated the Chimera myth is a hypothesis. [D] An existing myth could have been attached to a landscape that seemed perfectly suited to it.

Killing Chimera does not end the king’s attempts

Bellerophon returns alive. The Lycian king sends him against the Solymi. He wins. He is sent against the Amazons. He wins again. Finally the king places elite Lycian warriors in ambush. Bellerophon kills them as well. [A]

Only then does the king conclude that the young man must truly be favored by the gods. He gives Bellerophon his daughter, a share of royal authority, and excellent land. [A] The man who arrived carrying his own death letter becomes a royal son-in-law and landholder.

If the story ended here, it would be a nearly perfect heroic fairy tale.

Homer suddenly changes tone.

When Bellerophon becomes hated by all the gods, he wanders alone over the Aleian plain, devouring his own spirit and avoiding human paths. [A]

Homer does not tell us exactly why.

Later poets explain the silence: he flew too high

Pindar and later traditions connect Bellerophon’s fall with an attempt to rise on Pegasus into the realm of the gods. [A/C] He has defeated Chimera, survived war, defeated the Amazons, escaped assassination, married into royalty, and acquired land. Almost nothing remains in the mortal world capable of proving him further.

So he looks upward.

Later retellings add a dramatic detail: Zeus sends a gadfly to sting Pegasus, causing Bellerophon to fall. [C] This is not Homer’s version; Homer knows only divine hatred and lonely wandering.

Heracles reached Olympus. Why does Bellerophon fall?

The comparison with Heracles is revealing. Heracles ultimately enters Olympus; Bellerophon attempts to approach Olympus and is cast down. The distinction is not simply strength.

In the narrative logic of later tradition, Heracles undergoes suffering and death before being received among the gods. Bellerophon, still living as a mortal, attempts to cross the boundary through his own heroic capacity. One is admitted. The other attempts to enter by his own ascent. [D]

This has often been read through the Greek idea of hybris. Yet hybris should not be reduced to the modern English word “pride.” In ancient contexts it can involve violent arrogance, outrage, and transgression of social or divine limits. [D]

Gilgamesh also reaches a boundary he cannot cross

The Mesopotamian Gilgamesh defeats Humbaba and the Bull of Heaven and possesses extraordinary divine ancestry and physical strength. When Enkidu dies, however, Gilgamesh discovers that he too will die. He journeys to the edge of the world seeking immortality and ultimately returns without eternal life. [A]

There is no evidence that Bellerophon’s fall is a direct adaptation of Gilgamesh. [D] Yet the comparison is useful: exceptional heroes reach beyond normal human limits and then desire what mortals do not possess. Gilgamesh seeks immortality; Bellerophon seeks the realm of the gods.

Their endings differ. Gilgamesh returns to Uruk. Bellerophon becomes isolated from human society.

What about the Tower of Babel?

The later image of Bellerophon ascending toward divine space can recall the Tower of Babel in Genesis: a community builds upward toward heaven and seeks to make a name for itself, only for the project to collapse through the confusion of language. [A]

There is no evidence that the two stories are versions of one tradition. [D] Bellerophon is an individual hero; Babel concerns collective urban society. What can be compared is the broader theme of humans approaching a heavenly realm and encountering a boundary. The crucial question is not merely whether a mortal reaches upward, but who is entitled to cross the human-divine threshold, and by what process?

Pegasus remains in heaven while the rider falls

After Bellerophon, Pegasus remains associated with Zeus. Hesiod says that the winged horse dwells with Zeus and carries thunder and lightning. [A] Symbolically, the ending is almost too perfect. A supernatural horse born from Medusa accompanies a mortal hero in the defeat of Chimera. But when the mortal tries to pass into the divine realm, their destinies separate.

Bellerophon falls to earth.

Pegasus remains above.

One might say that the horse received the immortality of memory its rider tried to seize for himself. [D]

Follow Chimera’s family, and another monster is waiting

Within the monster genealogies surrounding Chimera, another composite creature appears: a woman’s face, lion’s body, and bird’s wings.

The Sphinx.

Yet the Sphinx was not first invented by Greek storytellers. More than a millennium before the classical Greek texts, Egypt already possessed monumental royal sphinxes combining human head and lion body. The Great Sphinx of Giza belongs to the Old Kingdom. [A]

But Egyptian and Greek sphinxes perform very different roles. Egyptian royal sphinx imagery strongly associates the creature with kingship and protection. The Greek Sphinx is typically female, winged, dangerous, and positioned outside Thebes to kill those who cannot answer her question. A visual form can travel while its story is transformed. [D]

One day a man approaches the Sphinx.

He does not defeat her by strength.

He answers a question.

His name is Oedipus.

He is intelligent enough to solve the most famous riddle in Greek myth, yet he does not know who his own parents are.

And the thread planted in the Perseus episode—the attempt to escape prophecy becomes the road by which prophecy is fulfilled—will return in its most devastating form.
