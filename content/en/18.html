EPISODE 18 · MEDEA

Was Medea’s Most Famous Ending Always the Same Story?

Euripides’ Medea — oath, exile, royal marriage, and the tragedy that reshaped a myth

By the time Jason and Medea reached Corinth, they were no longer the young hero and princess who had set out with the Golden Fleece before them. Jason had survived but failed to secure Iolcus; Medea had left father and homeland for him and become an outsider once more. The question left by the previous episode was therefore simple: what did Jason owe the person who had abandoned everything for his cause? Euripides’ Medea, first produced at Athens in 431 BCE, begins after that bond has already broken. [A]

Jason is arranging a new marriage with the daughter of Creon, king of Corinth. For Medea this is not merely romantic betrayal. Having severed her original kinship network, her marriage and children form the core of her place in the new city. Jason’s royal marriage therefore rearranges not just affection but oath, descent, status, and political power. [A/D]

Why does Jason not think of himself as a traitor?

Euripides makes Jason disturbing precisely because he offers reasons. He argues that marriage into the Corinthian royal house will improve his position and ultimately benefit his children. [A] Medea opens a different ledger: she recalls who enabled the Colchian trials, who abandoned homeland and family, and what promises made those sacrifices meaningful. [A] Jason calculates future advantage; Medea calculates unpaid debts from the past. [D]

Creon fears Medea’s competence

Creon orders Medea and her children into exile because he fears what an intelligent woman with unusual skills may do to his daughter. [A] The same knowledge has changed names throughout the saga. In Colchis it saved Jason; in Iolcus it became a weapon against Pelias; in Corinth it is treated as a security threat. [D] Medea asks for one final day and receives it. During that interval she secures refuge from Aegeus, king of Athens, and begins to arrange her retaliation. [A]

When a gift becomes a weapon

Medea pretends to accept Jason’s new marriage and sends splendid gifts to the princess through the children. The gifts become the mechanism of a fatal reversal that destroys the princess and Creon. [A] Euripides does not stage the event directly; a messenger narrates it afterward. [A] The theatrical convention does not diminish the horror so much as force the audience to construct it in imagination. [D]

The episode also resonates with the wider Greek danger of gifts that create obligations while concealing catastrophe. Pandora, too, belonged to a mythic vocabulary in which something presented as a gift could carry ruin. This is not evidence of a direct textual genealogy, but it shows how exchange in ancient narrative is rarely neutral. [D]

Was the most famous scene also the oldest?

After the collapse of Creon’s household, Medea chooses the most devastating possible revenge against Jason and makes their children part of the destruction. Euripides dwells on her hesitation and on her awareness of the enormity of the choice. [A] That self-awareness is essential: this is not a catastrophe produced by ignorance.

Evidence levels matter here. Euripides’ surviving play clearly attests the version in which Medea herself bears direct responsibility for the children’s deaths as revenge against Jason. [A] Yet we cannot say that this was the single original form of the myth. Pausanias preserves a different Corinthian tradition in which Medea attempted to make the children immortal by hiding them in Hera’s sanctuary, with disastrous results. [B/C] Other traditions associated the Corinthians themselves with the children’s deaths. The often-repeated claim that Euripides personally invented the filicide is therefore a scholarly hypothesis, not a demonstrated fact. [D]

What can be said securely is that Euripides placed this version at the center of a major tragedy in 431 BCE and that it became enormously influential. By about 400 BCE, a South Italian Lucanian red-figure calyx-krater now in the Cleveland Museum of Art shows Medea in the dragon chariot above the shattered household of Jason. [A: artifact] The vase is powerful evidence for the rapid migration of tragic Medea into visual culture.

Is the chariot an escape, an endorsement, or a final separation?

Medea does not leave as an ordinary fugitive. As a descendant of Helios, she is carried beyond Jason’s reach in a dragon-drawn chariot. [A] It would be too simple to read the divine vehicle as proof that the gods approve every act that preceded it. Divine assistance and moral endorsement are not identical propositions. [D]

The vertical staging nevertheless reverses the old Argonautic hierarchy. Jason, once the hero around whom the expedition was organized, remains below; Medea, once the indispensable helper, speaks from a position he cannot reach. [D] Jason has acquired neither Iolcus, nor a stable Corinthian dynasty, nor the future he imagined. Medea, too, has paid a cost that makes the word victory deeply unstable.

How far can comparison with neighboring traditions go?

Mesopotamian, Hittite, Hurrian, Egyptian, and Hebrew traditions contain their own combinations of dynastic conflict, dangerous vows, threatened children, and sanctions attached to broken oaths. The Hebrew story of Jephthah binds a vow to the fate of a daughter [A], while Near Eastern treaty traditions frequently place oath-breaking under divine sanction. [A] None of this proves that Medea’s plot was borrowed from a single eastern prototype. [D] The safer comparison is structural: oath, descent, and household continuity carried political and religious weight far beyond private emotion.

Royal marriages in Egypt and the ancient Near East are also historically documented instruments of diplomacy. [A: historical texts] A princess could embody an alliance, and children could embody its future. Medea and Jason should not be reduced to a disguised historical treaty marriage, but Jason’s attempt to gain a new political foundation through marriage would have sounded intelligible in ancient societies where marriage could reorganize power. [D]

Did Medea simply become a monster?

Euripides refuses that easy exit. Medea’s betrayal and precarious status are real within the play, but they do not morally erase the extremity of her revenge. Conversely, the horror of her retaliation does not erase Jason’s broken oath or his self-justifying logic. [A/D] Tragedy keeps both truths in view and denies the audience a clean moral accounting.

That may be why the story survived long after dragons and the Golden Fleece ceased to be its main attraction. How do people calculate sacrifice when a relationship collapses? How far can a new future cancel an old promise? Does suffering grant any right to unlimited revenge? If destroying an enemy also destroys part of one’s own life, can the result still be called victory? Those questions remain after the magic is stripped away. [D]

Medea’s route, however, is not finished. Ancient tradition takes her to Athens and links her with Aegeus. There she will encounter another young hero, the man who will eventually enter the Minotaur’s labyrinth: Theseus. Before the Argonautic generation has fully disappeared, the road to the next great Athenian hero already crosses Medea’s path.
