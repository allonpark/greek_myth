EPISODE 32 · ILIAD III–IV

The War Could Have Ended — So Why Did the Gods Start It Again?

Paris and Menelaus, Helen on the wall, and the broken oath — mortal peace inside a divine war

In the previous episode, the Achaean army nearly launched its ships for home after Zeus’ false dream and Agamemnon’s disastrous test of morale. Odysseus restored order, Thersites was beaten into silence, and the two armies again faced one another on the Trojan plain. Yet Iliad Book 3 refuses the expected mass collision. Instead, the war is suddenly compressed into the bodies of the two men most directly tied to its cause: Paris and Menelaus. They will fight once, and the result is supposed to end everything. [A]

Paris steps before the Trojan line in a leopard skin, carrying bow, sword, and spears. But when Menelaus recognizes him and leaps down to confront him, Paris retreats into the ranks. Hector savages his brother for possessing beauty without the courage expected of a warrior. [A] The two faces of Paris seen earlier in this series now meet in a single scene: the beautiful prince favored in the Judgment on Ida, and the fighter who recoils from the husband whose household he violated. Paris does not deny the charge completely. He insists that Aphrodite’s gifts should not be despised because gifts of the gods cannot simply be chosen, then proposes single combat for Helen and the property taken with her. [A]

The terms are strikingly explicit. The victor will take Helen and the treasure; everyone else will swear friendship and return home. [A] This matters because the Trojan War is often remembered as an unstoppable fate. Homer instead allows mortals to construct an actual procedure for peace: terms are stated, sacrificial victims are brought, leaders confirm the settlement, and gods are invoked as witnesses. [A] Peace is not a vague wish here. It becomes a binding arrangement that nearly succeeds.

Homer then moves the viewpoint from the battlefield to Troy’s wall. Iris summons Helen to the Scaean Gate, where Priam and the Trojan elders watch the plain. The old men acknowledge her godlike beauty but also wish that she might leave rather than remain a source of suffering. Priam seats Helen beside him and asks her to identify Agamemnon, Odysseus, Ajax, and other Achaean leaders. [A] A war fought ‘for Helen’ is thus viewed for a moment through Helen herself, who must identify the world she left behind among the enemy formations below.

A quieter tragedy follows. Helen looks for her brothers Castor and Polydeuces and wonders why she cannot see them, imagining that shame may have kept them from the expedition. The narrator tells the audience what Helen does not know: they are already buried in their homeland of Lacedaemon. [A] The detail belongs to mythic genealogy, but it also dramatizes the temporal violence of separation. Someone who has been away for years may not even know what has vanished at home. [D]

Priam comes down to the plain and the armies seal their oath with sacrifice. Agamemnon invokes Zeus, the Sun, rivers, earth, and powers that punish oath-breakers. [A] The slaughtered victims are not decorative ceremony. They can be read as a visible enactment of the destruction that should fall upon whoever violates the agreement. [D] That makes Book 4’s rupture far more serious than a mere tactical resumption of combat: mortals have called gods to witness a peace, and divine action will then help destroy it.

The duel begins. Paris’ spear fails against Menelaus’ shield. Menelaus’ spear pierces Paris’ shield and armor, though Paris escapes the point. Menelaus’ sword then shatters against the Trojan’s helmet. Finally he grabs Paris by the helmet crest and drags him toward the Achaean lines while the chinstrap tightens around Paris’ throat. [A] In military terms, the contest appears decided.

Then Aphrodite intervenes. She breaks the helmet strap, leaving Menelaus holding an empty helmet, wraps Paris in mist, and carries him into his fragrant bedroom inside Troy. [A] Menelaus searches the battlefield for an opponent who has simply vanished. Even the Trojans cannot hide Paris for him; Homer says they hate him like death. [A] The simple image of a city united behind Paris’ romance collapses. The Trojans themselves know the price his choices have imposed on them. [D]

Aphrodite next orders Helen to join Paris. Helen recognizes the goddess and resists, bitterly asking whether Aphrodite means to carry her off somewhere else and telling the goddess, in effect, to become Paris’ wife herself. [A] Aphrodite does not answer with gentle persuasion. She threatens Helen with catastrophic hatred and destruction if she refuses. [A] The question raised earlier in this series — whether Helen left willingly or under compulsion — returns in a sharper form. Homer’s Helen is an agent with judgment and resistance, yet she also lives under coercive divine power. [D]

Helen goes to the chamber but does not console Paris. She tells him he should have died at Menelaus’ hands and mocks his defeat. Paris replies that Athena helped Menelaus and that another contest might end differently, then turns immediately to desire. [A] Thousands wait outside for the outcome of a duel while the man at its center has been extracted by a goddess and placed in a bedroom. Homer’s contrast between Paris’ beauty and his deficiencies as a warrior could hardly be sharper. [D]

On the plain, Agamemnon declares Menelaus the victor and demands Helen, the treasure, and compensation. [A] The war could now end. Paris has disappeared from the contest, Menelaus controls the field, and both armies have sworn the settlement. Book 4, however, opens not among mortals but in an Olympian council. Zeus raises the possibility that Menelaus has won and that Helen might be restored while Troy survives. [A] Hera refuses. She wants not merely Helen’s return but Troy’s destruction. [A]

This radically complicates responsibility. Paris’ actions remain a human cause of war; so do Agamemnon’s ambitions, the economy of plunder, and heroic competition for honor. But when mortals construct a workable peace, divine interests overturn it. [A/D] In Homer, ‘the god caused it’ and ‘the human is responsible’ are not mutually exclusive explanations. Causation is layered.

Zeus finally sends Athena down. She approaches the Trojan archer Pandarus and tempts him with the glory he could win by shooting Menelaus. Pandarus draws and releases. [A] Yet Athena does not permit the arrow to kill its target. She deflects it enough to prevent a fatal wound. [A] A god prompts the human act that breaks the oath and simultaneously controls its consequence. Pandarus genuinely releases the bowstring, but the audience knows that Athena stands behind the decision. This double causality is one of the central difficulties — and powers — of Homeric theology. [D]

When Menelaus bleeds, Agamemnon fears that his brother may die, but the wound is not fatal and the physician Machaon treats it. [A] The arrow’s political effect matters more than its physical damage. The sworn peace has been violated. The armies rearm, and war resumes. Thousands do not need to decide independently to abandon peace; one arrow is enough to recreate the conditions of collective violence. [D]

The representative image for this episode is Louvre G115, an Attic red-figure kylix painted by Douris and made by Kalliades, about 490–480 BCE. It is a genuine ancient artifact and shows Menelaus pursuing Paris while Aphrodite and Artemis stand to either side. [A: artifact] It is not a photograph of a Bronze Age Trojan War. It is direct evidence that early fifth-century Greek viewers recognized and visualized this duel in a developed mythic tradition. [A/D]

Comparison with the Hittite world makes the oath especially revealing. Late Bronze Age Hittite treaties invoke divine witnesses and attach curses to treaty violation, while the plague prayers of Mursili II search earlier oath-breaking as a cause of communal disaster. [A] There is no documentary chain proving that Homer copied a particular Hittite treaty. [D] The useful comparison is institutional: in the wider eastern Mediterranean and Anatolian world, political promises could be religious liabilities enforced under divine witness.

Hurrian traditions also connect divine conflict with the order of the human world, but no close counterpart is known in which two armies settle a war by duel and a deity then manipulates an archer into breaking the peace. [D] Mesopotamian cultures likewise provide extensive evidence for oaths, treaties, and divine sanctions, but that does not make Pandarus’ arrow a borrowed Mesopotamian motif. The stronger comparative point is that gods as guarantors of political obligation were not a uniquely Homeric idea. [A/D]

The Hebrew Bible likewise treats violated oaths and covenants as acts with religious and political consequences. In 2 Samuel 21, Saul’s breach against the Gibeonites is connected with a later famine. [A] There is no evidence that this is a source for Homer. [D] Egyptian international diplomacy offers an even more concrete parallel at the institutional level: the Egyptian-Hittite peace treaty associated with Ramesses II and Hattusili III invokes divine sanction in a real royal agreement. [A] Again, this illuminates the world of oath-bound diplomacy; it does not prove the historicity of Paris and Menelaus.

Archaeology requires the same discipline. Late Bronze Age fortifications and destruction levels at Hisarlik demonstrate a substantial settlement in northwestern Anatolia, and the identification of Hittite Wilusa with (W)Ilios has strong scholarly support. [D] None of those layers can verify that Menelaus fought Paris or that Pandarus broke a truce. The plausibility of a historical geopolitical background and the historicity of individual Homeric events are different evidentiary questions.

The central fact of this episode is therefore not simply that Menelaus won the duel. Mortals found a way to end the war. They sacrificed, negotiated terms, invoked gods, and reached a result. Then Aphrodite rescued Paris, coerced Helen, the Olympians debated whether Troy should survive, and Athena used Pandarus’ hand to destroy the settlement. [A] War in the Iliad is sustained neither by human desire alone nor by divine rivalry alone. Each becomes a cause of the other. [D]

Now the war truly opens. Achilles remains by his ships, Menelaus is wounded, and Pandarus’ arrow has ended the truce. The next center of gravity is a younger warrior we have not yet followed closely. His father is Tydeus, Athena grants him extraordinary favor, and soon he will aim his weapon not only at Trojans but at gods themselves.

His name is Diomedes. The next episode enters the astonishing day of Iliad Book 5, when a mortal warrior wounds the divine.
