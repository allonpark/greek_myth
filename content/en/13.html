EPISODE 13

Why Did Two Brothers Kill Each Other at the Same Gate?

Seven Against Thebes — how Oedipus’ family curse became a war for an entire city

At the end of the previous chapter, Oedipus discovered that the criminal he had spent the play hunting was himself. Yet the tragedy of Thebes did not end with the collapse of one king. From that point onward, the catastrophe moved out of one man’s body and spread across the city. Oedipus left two sons, Eteocles and Polynices. They inherited the same father, the same royal house, and a claim upon the same city. In the end they would stand at the same gate and raise weapons against one another. Their war became the Greek story remembered as the expedition of the Seven Against Thebes.

Calling it merely a civil war between brothers competing for a throne misses its oldest scale. In surviving archaic poetry, the Theban war already stands beside the Trojan War as one of the great catastrophes of the Heroic Age. In Works and Days, Hesiod says that part of the race of heroes was destroyed by grim war at “seven-gated Thebes,” fighting over the possessions of Oedipus, while another part died at Troy for Helen. [A] In this early Greek map of the mythical past, Thebes is not simply a prelude to Troy. It is one of the two great wars that consumed the heroes.

Oedipus left his sons an inheritance that could not be divided

The traditions disagree over the exact path by which the brothers became enemies. The familiar later version says that they agreed to rule in alternating years, but Eteocles refused to surrender the throne when his term ended and Polynices went into exile. That neat system of alternating kingship becomes especially clear in later mythographic and dramatic traditions. [C] The core assumed by Aeschylus’ Seven Against Thebes is harsher: Oedipus has cursed his sons so that they will divide their inheritance by the sword, and Polynices returns against his homeland with an Argive army. [A]

The inheritance is more than property. Kingship, ancestral house, civic cults, and the father’s name are fused together. Neither brother wants half of Thebes because kingship cannot simply be cut into equal parcels. Oedipus’ legacy therefore destroys his sons not because it is abundant, but because its central object is indivisible. Near the end of Aeschylus’ play, the chorus imagines iron itself—the weapon—as the savage foreign “divider” that apportions their inheritance. [A] The only land the brothers finally divide equally is the amount of earth required for their graves.

The exiled prince returned from Argos carrying a war

In traditions systematized later, Polynices reaches Argos, becomes the son-in-law of King Adrastus, and helps organize an expedition to recover his position. [C] Lists of the Seven vary across authors, but in Aeschylus’ tragedy of 467 BCE the attackers are Tydeus, Capaneus, Eteoclus, Hippomedon, Parthenopaeus, Amphiaraus, and Polynices, each directed toward one of Thebes’ seven gates. [A]

Aeschylus does not immediately stage a conventional battle. Instead, a scout returns from outside the walls and describes each enemy champion. Eteocles then assigns a Theban defender to oppose him. The sequence makes the war resemble a terrifying board of matched pieces. A warrior’s temperament, the emblem on his shield, his boasts, and his attitude toward the gods become signs of what may happen before combat begins. [A] A shield here is not merely armor. It is a portable declaration of the identity a warrior wishes the world to see.

The most dangerous voice belongs to Capaneus. He boasts that he will destroy Thebes whether the gods approve or not, and treats the thunder and lightning of Zeus as though they were no more threatening than the heat of midday. His shield bears a man with fire and a declaration that he will burn the city. [A] Eteocles hears in the boast the prediction of Capaneus’ own defeat. Later tradition makes the image still more famous: Capaneus climbs the wall and is struck down by Zeus’ thunderbolt. [C] The episode became an exceptionally clear image of what later readers call hybris—human self-assertion swelling beyond its mortal boundary. [D]

The warrior who joined the expedition while condemning it

The strangest of the Seven is the seer Amphiaraus. Aeschylus’ scout calls him measured, just, courageous, and reverent toward the gods. His shield carries no spectacular emblem. He does not want to seem the best; he wants to be the best. [A] More strikingly, he criticizes his own side. He asks Polynices how it can be just to attack the city of his ancestors and its native gods with a foreign army. [A]

Later traditions develop the story that Amphiaraus knew the expedition would fail and that he himself would die, while his wife Eriphyle was induced by treasure to decide that he must go. [C] But Aeschylus already poses the deeper paradox: can a just individual escape the disaster of an unjust collective once he joins it? Eteocles remarks that a pious man sailing with criminal companions may perish with the whole ship. [A] The Theban war therefore refuses to become a simple contest between good and evil. The invading army contains a man who condemns its cause, while the king defending Thebes is himself trapped in a violent and cursed family history.

At the seventh gate, the war becomes a family story again

After the scout has named six attackers, he comes to the seventh. At the seventh gate stands Polynices. His shield shows an armed man led by a woman who identifies herself as Dikē—Justice. The message is that she will bring this man home and restore him to his father’s halls. [A] To Polynices, the expedition is not invasion but restoration of a violated right. To Eteocles inside the walls, Polynices is a man who has brought foreigners to attack his own country.

That is where the war’s hardest question emerges: whose justice? To the exile, justice means return. To the ruler defending the walls, justice means defense of the city. Aeschylus does not resolve the dispute like a court case. Eteocles refuses to send another champion. He will meet his brother himself. The chorus begs him not to go, recognizing the approaching fulfillment of Oedipus’ curse, but Eteocles will not turn aside. [A]

The brothers kill one another. [A] There is no victor. Eteocles saves the city but does not return alive to rule it; Polynices kills his brother but never recovers his home. Oedipus’ curse achieves a terrible equality. Instead of one son possessing everything, both lose everything at the same instant.

Why seven? The gates matter more than numerology

That Thebes was an important Bronze Age center is archaeologically secure. The Cadmea has yielded Mycenaean palatial remains and Linear B documents. [A: archaeology] But the mythical “seven gates” cannot simply be mapped onto a securely reconstructed Mycenaean defensive plan. Classical texts and later travelers preserve gate names, but the lists and locations do not align perfectly. [D] The number seven should therefore not be treated as if the poem were a Bronze Age engineering report.

Yet seven is structurally crucial in Aeschylus. Seven attackers face seven defenders, and the sequence narrows until the final pair are brothers. [A/D] A war involving an entire city is compressed back into the fracture of one household. The stage structure itself demonstrates that the public war is the enlarged form of a private dynastic wound.

Sibling violence around the ancient world: comparison without false ancestry

Violence between brothers or close kin is not uniquely Greek. In the Hebrew Bible, Cain and Abel provide one of antiquity’s most famous narratives of one brother killing another, but their conflict is neither a dynastic civil war nor a struggle in which the survivor becomes the heroic defender of a city. [A] The structural resemblance—brother shedding brother’s blood—is real, but the political symmetry of the Theban war is absent. There is no evidence that one story was directly borrowed from the other. [D]

Egyptian traditions surrounding Osiris, Seth, and Horus connect kinship conflict much more directly with kingship. After the death of Osiris, the right to rule becomes the subject of prolonged conflict between Seth and Horus, preserved in different forms in Egyptian texts. [A] But this is not a prototype for Eteocles and Polynices. The Egyptian dispute concerns the succession to a dead king and the cosmic legitimacy of royal inheritance; the Theban brothers belong to the same generation and destroy one another over the same living city.

Hurrian-Hittite succession mythology, encountered earlier in this series through Anu, Kumarbi, and the Storm-god, likewise imagines sovereignty passing through violent conflict among divine generations. [A] Those traditions are more directly comparable to the Uranus–Cronus–Zeus succession than to the Theban civil war. The broader comparative point is political rather than genealogical: ancient societies often imagined kingship as inseparable from family. [D] Blood relationship does not guarantee peace. When blood legitimizes rule, the question of which relative is the rightful heir can transform a household dispute into a war involving an entire state.

Thebes and Troy: the two wars that consume the heroes

We can now recover a thread planted earlier in the series. Hesiod places Thebes and Troy in the same explanation of the end of the heroic race. Some heroes die at seven-gated Thebes; others cross the sea and die at Troy. [A] By roughly the eighth or seventh century BCE, therefore, poetic tradition already treated the two wars as major coordinates in the imagined history of the heroes.

But one must stop before claiming that “Zeus caused the Theban War to reduce the human population.” The explicit motif of a divine plan that relieves the burden of humanity through war is much clearer in ancient testimony and fragments concerning the lost Cypria, in the background to the Trojan War. [B] In Mesopotamian Atrahasis, divine responses to human multiplication and noise lead to plague, drought, and finally flood; in later Greek epic tradition, vast heroic warfare can become entangled with the transition between human ages. No direct line of borrowing between these specific mechanisms has been demonstrated. They are better treated as comparable ancient answers to the problem of human excess and restored cosmic order. [D]

The war ends, and another tragedy begins

Thebes is not captured. Both royal brothers, however, are dead. The transmitted ending of Aeschylus’ play then introduces another problem. Eteocles is to receive burial as defender of the city, while Polynices is denied burial as its attacker, and Antigone declares that she will resist. [A] Modern textual scholarship has long debated whether parts of this final burial conflict belonged to Aeschylus’ original 467 BCE performance or were expanded later under the influence of the Antigone tradition familiar from Sophocles. [D]

That uncertainty makes the next question more, not less, interesting. In Greek religion, burial is not merely a family preference. It is an obligation connecting the dead, the household, the community, and the gods. Can the state continue punishing a traitor after death? Can a family duty be older and stronger than a king’s decree? What happens when civic law and divine obligation point in opposite directions?

The spears of the two brothers have stopped at the gate, but the conflict of Thebes now moves to a different frontier: the living and the dead, the law of the city and the law claimed for the gods. The next person to stand against a ruler will not be one of the Seven. She will be a young woman with no army behind her. Her name is Antigone.
