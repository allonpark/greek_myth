EPISODE 10 · HERACLES

Stronger Than Monsters, Defeated by Himself

Why did the Twelve Labors begin, and did the ancient Greek word “hero” mean a morally good person?

Follow the lineage of Perseus and eventually another child appears: son of Zeus and the mortal Alcmene, the man later recognizable across the Greek world by lion skin and club—Heracles. Modern readers easily turn him into an ancient superhero: the strongest mortal, killer of the Nemean Lion, destroyer of the Hydra, conqueror who descends to the underworld and brings Cerberus back alive.

But the Twelve Labors do not begin because Heracles wants adventure or dreams of saving the world. In the most familiar mythographic sequence, they are connected with atonement for the most terrible act of his life. The story is darker than a catalogue of monsters.

Hera’s struggle begins before Heracles is born

The Iliad already knows a story in which Zeus declares that a descendant of Perseus about to be born will rule those around him, only for Hera to manipulate the timing of births. Eurystheus is born first and Heracles delayed. [A] The result is that the enormously powerful Heracles will later serve a much weaker ruler.

Later tradition has Hera send two snakes to kill Heracles in his cradle. The infant strangles them with his bare hands. [C] From the beginning, ordinary human physical limits do not apply to him.

Even his name is paradoxical: Herakles is traditionally connected with “glory of Hera.” The goddess who opposes him throughout life is embedded in the name by which his fame survives. The irony will be resolved only at the end.

Before the labors came a family catastrophe

Heracles grows, aids Thebes, marries Megara, and establishes a family. Then madness overwhelms him. Later traditions attribute it to Hera; in Euripides’ fifth-century tragedy Heracles, the personified Madness, Lyssa, arrives under divine command. [A/C]

Heracles believes he is attacking enemies. When sanity returns, he discovers that the victims were his own family. No lion or serpent stands before him now—only the irreversible result of what his own hands have done.

The chronology varies by source. In the familiar later mythographic arrangement, the murders lead to purification and service under Eurystheus. [C] Euripides deliberately places the family killing after the labors, reshaping the traditional sequence for tragedy. [A] Even antiquity did not possess one fixed biography of Heracles.

Must a man atone for an act committed in madness?

A modern reader may ask whether Heracles is not himself a victim. If he kills while insane, why must he be purified? Modern legal concepts of criminal responsibility do not map neatly onto ancient Greek religious ideas. Killing could produce pollution—often discussed through the concept of miasma—whose consequences were not erased simply by lack of conscious intention. [A/D]

Greek tragedy repeatedly insists that ignorance does not undo results. Oedipus will kill his father without knowing who he is and marry his mother without recognizing her. The lack of knowledge deepens the tragedy rather than deleting what happened.

Heracles cannot use strength to reverse the past. He therefore enters a path of purification and service, ultimately taking commands from Eurystheus.

The labors were not always a perfectly fixed list of twelve

We speak casually of “the Twelve Labors,” but individual adventures of Heracles appear early and separately in poetry and visual art. The exact canonical list developed across a long history of transmission. [A/B/C/D]

Later mythographic tradition explains that ten tasks were originally required, but Eurystheus rejected two. The Hydra did not count because Iolaus assisted; the Augean stables did not count because Heracles sought payment. Two extra labors were therefore imposed. [C]

The strongest man in the world can return from killing a monster and still hear a king say: “That one does not count.” Physical power does not control the rules under which it is judged.

The Nemean Lion — the enemy becomes the hero’s armor

The first famous labor is the Nemean Lion. Later tradition makes its hide impervious or exceptionally resistant to ordinary weapons. Heracles ultimately grapples with the lion in close quarters and strangles it. [C]

He then wears the skin. Ancient pottery and sculpture make this one of the strongest visual identifiers in Greek art: a man under a lion scalp and hide can be recognized as Heracles before any inscription is read. [A: surviving art] The monster’s body becomes the hero’s identity.

The Hydra — his death is already hidden inside his victory

The Lernaean Hydra is a many-headed serpent whose severed heads regenerate. The precise number of heads varies across textual and visual traditions. [A/C]

In later accounts, Heracles cuts while his nephew Iolaus cauterizes the neck-stumps to stop regrowth. The final immortal head is buried under a rock. [C] Heracles then dips his arrows into the Hydra’s venom.

A long narrative fuse is lit. Those poisoned arrows will later kill the centaur Nessus. Nessus’s contaminated blood will reach Heracles’ wife Deianeira and, much later, destroy Heracles himself. The hero acquires the instrument of his own death while completing one of his greatest victories.

Some labors are about restraint rather than killing

The Ceryneian Hind is sacred and should not simply be slaughtered; the Erymanthian Boar must be captured alive. [C] The challenge is no longer merely violence. The strongest man must sometimes succeed by controlling strength.

The Augean stables provide an even more famous change of method. Faced with an impossible quantity of accumulated filth, Heracles does not shovel it out by hand. He redirects river water through the stables. [C] The solution changes the system rather than applying more muscle.

The map of the labors expands toward the edges of the known world

Through the Stymphalian Birds, the Cretan Bull, the man-eating horses of Diomedes, and the girdle of the Amazon queen Hippolyta, the geography expands beyond one Greek locality. In the expedition for Geryon’s cattle, later tradition places Heracles at the far western edge of the Mediterranean and links him with the “Pillars of Heracles,” later associated with the Strait of Gibraltar. [C]

Myth becomes geography. The limit of the familiar world receives the name of the hero believed to have traveled furthest.

Atlas and the golden apples — the strongest man uses a trick

In the labor of the Hesperides, the Titan Atlas returns. Later tradition has Heracles temporarily bear the sky while Atlas obtains the golden apples. When Atlas returns and prefers not to resume his burden, Heracles pretends to agree, asking Atlas to take the sky for only a moment while he adjusts his support. Atlas accepts. Heracles picks up the apples and leaves. [C]

The world’s strongest hero wins here not through strength but through cunning—the same indirect logic we saw in Perseus.

The final labor crosses the boundary of death

For the last great task, Heracles must descend to Hades and bring Cerberus back alive. [C] A living being’s descent into the realm of the dead is often called a katabasis, a pattern that later becomes central to stories of Orpheus, Odysseus, and Aeneas.

Mesopotamia possessed much older traditions of divine descent, especially Inanna/Ishtar’s journey to the underworld. [A] The plots are very different, yet the idea that a living divine or heroic being can cross the boundary of death and return has deep roots across ancient West Asia. A direct line of borrowing cannot simply be assumed. [D]

Prometheus’s ancient punishment ends here

One of the long threads from Episode 6 is finally recovered. Prometheus still hangs bound while an eagle consumes his liver. Hesiod knows a tradition in which Heracles kills the eagle and frees him, with Zeus permitting the act in a way that contributes to the fame of his son. [A]

The Titan who gave fire to humanity is released generations later by a mortal son of Zeus. A conflict that began in the age of divine kingship is partially resolved in the age of heroes.

The poison hidden in an old victory comes back

Heracles eventually marries Deianeira. When the centaur Nessus attempts to harm her, Heracles shoots him with an arrow poisoned long ago in the Hydra’s venom. [A/C]

Dying, Nessus lies: his blood, he says, can serve as a love charm. Years later, fearing that Heracles loves another woman, Deianeira applies the blood to a garment. When Heracles wears it, the poison burns into his flesh. He cannot simply tear the cloth away without tearing himself apart.

The man who defeated lions, serpents, monsters, warriors, and the boundary of death is destroyed by the poison he collected during his second labor. His triumph had carried its own future catastrophe inside it.

Heracles dies — and is received among the gods

Unable to endure the agony, Heracles mounts a funeral pyre. Later tradition separates mortal and divine aspects: the mortal part burns, while the divine part rises to Olympus. [C] There he is reconciled with Hera and marries her daughter Hebe.

Only at the end does the meaning “glory of Hera” become fully ironic and complete.

The ancient Greek hero is not necessarily a morally perfect person in the modern superhero sense. Heracles rages, kills, makes terrible mistakes, and bears consequences. Yet his deeds exceed ordinary human limits, and he becomes an object of hero cult and later divine honor. [A/D]

And now a creature born long ago from Medusa’s severed neck returns.

Pegasus.

His defining rider is not Perseus but Bellerophon. Bellerophon will kill the fire-breathing Chimera, survive impossible assignments, and become so successful that he eventually looks upward.

Why not ride all the way to Olympus?

The next episode begins when a victorious mortal climbs too high.
