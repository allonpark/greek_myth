EPISODE 07 · PANDORA'S JAR

Where Did Human Suffering Come From, and Was What Remained Really Hope?

The ancient text behind “Pandora’s box,” and the unresolved riddle of Elpis

In the previous episode, Prometheus restored fire to humanity. The fire remained; Zeus did not permanently take it back. Instead he prepared a punishment designed to remain within human society far longer than a confiscated flame. Hephaestus formed the figure of a woman from earth and water; Athena gave skill and clothing; Aphrodite contributed beauty and desire; Hermes added persuasive, deceptive speech and a cunning disposition. [A] Because the gods contributed gifts, she was called Pandora.

In Hesiod’s story, however, Pandora is not created simply to bless humanity. She belongs to Zeus’s retaliation for the theft of fire—a beautiful punishment. [A] A visibly monstrous gift might have been rejected. Pandora succeeds precisely because danger arrives in an attractive form.

Epimetheus accepted the gift after being warned

Prometheus warns his brother Epimetheus not to accept any gift from Zeus. Epimetheus receives Pandora anyway and only afterward remembers the warning. [A] The traditional contrast between “Forethought” and “Afterthought” becomes almost the structure of the plot.

With Pandora comes a vessel that modern culture almost automatically calls a box. Hesiod’s Greek does not say “box.” The word is pithos, a large storage jar of the kind used for grain, oil, or wine. [A] The familiar image of “Pandora’s box” is the product of much later translation and reception history. [C/D]

Why did Pandora open it?

Children’s retellings often say that Pandora was explicitly forbidden to open the container but could not resist her curiosity. Hesiod does not build the episode around that psychological drama. The prohibition and the emphasis on irresistible curiosity are later developments. [C]

In Works and Days, the central issue is how Zeus’s plan changes the condition of human life. Once the jar is opened, sufferings spread through the mortal world. Hesiod says that before this, humans lived apart from painful toil, misery, and illnesses that bring rapid aging. [A] Disease now moves among humans day and night. Hesiod even imagines diseases as voiceless, approaching without warning.

This should not be modernized into an ancient theory of microbes or viruses. [D] What the poem captures is the frightening human experience of illness as something invisible and unpredictable.

One thing remained: Elpis

As the contents escape, one thing remains inside the jar: Elpis, usually translated as “Hope.” [A] Modern moral retellings therefore tend to produce a comforting conclusion: all evils entered the world, but hope remained for humankind.

Hesiod is not so clear. The Greek word elpis can mean expectation or anticipation more broadly, not only optimistic hope for a good future. [D] And there is a logical difficulty: if the things that leave the jar become available to humans, what does it mean that Elpis remains inside? Is hope preserved safely for humanity, or withheld from humanity?

Scholars have argued for several possibilities. Perhaps hope is protected so that humans can survive suffering. Perhaps foreknowledge of the future is denied, leaving mortals trapped in uncertainty. Perhaps expectation is itself ambiguous, sustaining life while also allowing people to endure misery for futures that may never come. [D] Hesiod never supplies a final interpretive key. The jar has remained open to debate for more than two millennia.

Is Pandora simply the Greek Eve?

The comparison with Eve in Genesis is tempting. A woman appears near the beginning of human life, and an event associated with her is followed by a more painful human condition. That structural resemblance is real enough to discuss. [D] Yet the details differ dramatically. Genesis centers on a serpent’s temptation, a divine prohibition, and the eating of forbidden fruit. Hesiod’s Pandora episode contains no forbidden fruit, no serpent tempter, and no explicit command not to open the jar. [A]

It is therefore too simple to call Pandora “the Greek Eve.” More useful is the observation that both traditions imagine present human hardship as something that was not always the natural condition of the world. An earlier state is lost, and a decisive primordial event explains why ordinary life now includes toil, suffering, and mortality.

In Mesopotamia, the human problem was noise rather than sin

The Atrahasis tradition gives a very different explanation. Humans were created to take over divine labor, but as they multiply their noise becomes intolerable to Enlil. Plague, drought, and famine are used to reduce their numbers; ultimately a flood is chosen. [A]

Greek myth connects suffering with conflict between Zeus and Prometheus. Genesis stresses disobedience and moral rupture. Atrahasis emphasizes human multiplication and disturbance. Similar questions do not necessarily produce similar theology.

After Pandora, Hesiod starts human history again

Once the Pandora episode is finished, Works and Days introduces another account of human decline: the races or ages of humanity. [A] First comes the Golden Race under Cronus, living far from exhausting labor and miserable old age. Then comes the Silver Race, followed by the Bronze Race, a violent people who ultimately destroy themselves.

At that point a simple metallic decline—gold, silver, bronze, iron—seems inevitable. Hesiod interrupts it with something unexpected.

The Heroic Race.

These heroes are described as better and more just than the Bronze Race. Hesiod explicitly associates them with two great wars: the war at seven-gated Thebes and the expedition to Troy over Helen. [A] For the first time, the remote world of Oedipus, Achilles, and Odysseus begins to enter the chronology we have been constructing.

The Iron Race — Hesiod’s own world

Last comes the Iron Race, and Hesiod says that this is the age in which he himself lives. He laments that he would rather have died before it or been born afterward. [A] Humans labor by day and suffer by night. Bonds between parents and children, brothers, hosts and guests deteriorate. Force and falsehood displace justice.

Eventually Aidos and Nemesis—figures associated with shame, moral restraint, and righteous indignation—will leave humanity and return to the gods, leaving mortals with suffering and little defense against evil. [A]

Pandora explains evil entering the human world. The Iron Age imagines the final restraints against evil leaving it. They are different stories, but both answer the same haunting question: why is human life now so difficult? [D]

Then the world is almost erased by water

The next story opens onto a catastrophe. Greek tradition preserves a flood in which humanity is nearly destroyed. Zeus decides upon destruction, but one man survives: Deucalion, son of Prometheus.

Far to the east, Mesopotamian clay tablets preserve much earlier written flood traditions: Atrahasis and later Utnapishtim. The gods decide upon a flood; a chosen survivor receives warning, builds a vessel, brings family and living creatures aboard, survives the waters, and makes sacrifice afterward. Genesis has Noah.

Now the parallels are far more specific than the broad motif of humans made from earth. Vessel, chosen survivor, family, animals, flood, testing for dry land, sacrifice, and a new post-flood order appear in recognizable sequences.

The next episode places the traditions on the same table.

Atrahasis. Noah. Deucalion.

Were these three tellings of one ancient flood—or three different histories carried by the same kind of story?
