EPISODE 26 · AT THE THRESHOLD OF TROY

Was Helen Abducted, or Did She Leave of Her Own Will?

The Oath of Tyndareus, Paris in Sparta, conflicting ancient Helens — and the moment a promise designed to prevent war bound Greece to one

At the end of the previous episode, Paris chose Aphrodite on Mount Ida. In later tradition the golden apple compresses that decision into a single glittering object, and Aphrodite’s reward is Helen, the most beautiful woman in the world. Yet if the next step is reduced to “Paris abducted Helen and the Greeks sailed to rescue her,” we lose a question that ancient authors themselves kept reopening for centuries: Was Helen carried away by force, did she leave under the power of Aphrodite and her own desire, or did she never reach Troy at all? The surviving evidence refuses to give one answer. The point is not to declare one version the authentic myth, but to watch successive poets, historians, and dramatists redistribute responsibility for the same catastrophe.

An oath invented to prevent war

Long before Paris reached Sparta, Helen’s marriage was already politically explosive. Fragments of the lost Hesiodic Catalogue of Women describe suitors coming from across the heroic world and Tyndareus requiring binding oaths concerning Helen’s marriage. Because the work survives only in fragments and ancient transmission, this evidence is [B]. The suitors swear that if anyone takes Helen by force, they will act together against the offender. Later Apollodorus adds Odysseus’ famous stratagem: Tyndareus fears that choosing one husband will make the rejected suitors fight, so Odysseus proposes that all swear in advance to defend the chosen bridegroom; in return, Tyndareus is to help Odysseus obtain Penelope. [C]

Menelaus becomes Helen’s husband, and at first the device works: armed aristocrats do not fight one another over the marriage. Its success, however, creates the machinery of the later disaster. Once Helen is taken from Menelaus, a domestic crisis becomes the shared obligation of multiple royal houses. [D] A compact created to contain violence becomes, when its trigger condition is met, a system for mobilizing violence. We should not turn a heroic oath into a modern alliance treaty, but the narrative paradox is striking: wars may expand not only because promises fail, but because promises are kept.

Paris did not enter Sparta as an enemy

In Proclus’ summary of the lost Cypria, Paris—Alexandros—builds ships and travels to Sparta. Menelaus receives him not as an enemy but as a guest. Menelaus then sails to Crete and instructs Helen to provide for the visitors. Aphrodite brings Helen and Alexandros together; they load substantial property aboard and sail away by night. Since the Cypria itself is lost and known here through an ancient summary, this is [B].

The crucial point is that Paris does not smash a gate and drag Helen from a burning palace in this recoverable version. He enters as a guest. Against the Greek institution of xenia, the reciprocal and religiously charged relationship between host and guest, his action becomes more than an erotic scandal. He leaves the household of the man who welcomed him with that man’s wife and property. [D] The moral grammar of the Trojan story therefore includes broken hospitality, broken marriage, and appropriation of wealth as well as desire.

Archaeology and Late Bronze Age documents independently establish intense movement, trade, diplomacy, gift exchange, and conflict between the Aegean and Anatolia. The identification of Hittite Ahhiyawa with an Achaean Greek sphere and Wilusa with Ilios/Troy has substantial scholarly support. [A: texts/archaeology; D: identification] But no contemporary document records a Mycenaean king’s wife fleeing with a Trojan prince. The reality of Bronze Age international contact and the historicity of Helen’s departure are separate evidentiary questions.

Homer’s Helen cannot be reduced to a passive abductee

The Iliad begins in the tenth year of the war and therefore does not narrate the original departure from Sparta. Yet Helen’s own speech at Troy resists a simple portrait of a woman who was merely carried away. In Book 3, standing on the walls with Priam, she identifies the Greek commanders and looks back with shame upon leaving husband, kin, and child; in Book 6 she again condemns her situation and herself. [A] At the same time, when Aphrodite commands her to go to Paris after the duel with Menelaus, Helen openly resists and insults the goddess before yielding under divine threat. [A]

Modern legal binaries of voluntary versus coerced action therefore map poorly onto Homeric divine causation. Helen remembers, judges, regrets, and speaks as someone implicated in her departure; yet Aphrodite is also an overwhelming supernatural force acting upon desire and conduct. [D] In epic, divine causation is neither merely a psychological metaphor nor a simple device that erases all human agency. Divine and human causes can occupy the same event.

Nor is Homer’s Helen simply a woman romantically devoted to Paris. After Paris survives his duel with Menelaus, she rebukes him and bitterly challenges his martial inadequacy. [A] Later romantic paintings of Paris and Helen capture only one of many possible receptions, not the full Homeric relationship.

Sappho shifts the weight of responsibility

In the early sixth century BCE, Sappho’s surviving Fragment 16 deploys Helen in a very different argument. Some call cavalry, infantry, or ships the most beautiful thing on earth; Sappho’s speaker says it is whatever one loves. Helen becomes the example: despite an excellent husband, she went to Troy, leaving parents and child behind. [A] Here she functions less as military booty than as proof that desire can overturn ordinary hierarchies of value.

That does not mean Sappho is making a modern declaration of autonomous romantic choice. Aphroditic desire remains a power capable of bending human judgment, and the line between agency and divine eros remains unstable. [D] But it decisively demonstrates that violent abduction was never the only ancient Greek way to imagine Helen’s departure.

Some Greeks said Helen never went to Troy

Herodotus’ Histories Book 2 gives an even more radical alternative, which he attributes to Egyptian priests. In his account, Paris and Helen are driven to Egypt by adverse weather. King Proteus learns that Paris has violated hospitality by taking another man’s wife and property; he detains Helen and the goods for Menelaus and expels Paris. When the Greeks later demand Helen at Troy, the Trojans insist that she is not there. The Greeks refuse to believe them and only discover the truth after destroying the city. [A]

Herodotus does more than report the story. He offers his own rationalizing argument: had Helen really been in Troy, Priam and the Trojans would not sensibly have sacrificed their sons and city indefinitely for one woman. [A] This is not modern archaeological proof; it is evidence that a fifth-century BCE historian was already testing mythic traditions against his own standards of political plausibility. [D]

Euripides pushes the alternative still further in his Helen of 412 BCE. The real Helen remains in Egypt, while Hera creates an eidolon, a phantom Helen, that goes to Troy. Greeks and Trojans spend ten years killing one another over an image. [A] In this version, the accusation of Helen’s sexual betrayal collapses: the woman for whom the war was supposedly fought was never there.

An earlier root lies in Stesichorus’ famous Palinode. The poem is lost as a complete work, but Plato and other ancient witnesses preserve the tradition and key wording: after blaming Helen and being struck blind, the poet retracts the story—she did not board the ships and did not reach Troy—and recovers his sight. [B] The exact literary genealogy from Stesichorus to Euripides remains debated, but the evidence shows that a “Helen never went to Troy” tradition existed well before Euripides.

Does the Egyptian Helen come from Egypt?

Herodotus’ claim that Egyptian priests told him the story does not automatically make it a pharaonic Egyptian myth. [D] What survives is Herodotus’ Greek text, and we cannot independently reconstruct how Egyptian temple lore, Greek communities in Egypt, and the historian’s own reasoning contributed to it. No Bronze Age Egyptian document has been found recording that Helen of Sparta was kept in Egypt while Greeks fought at Troy.

Egypt was, however, no imaginary blank space to archaic and classical Greeks. From the seventh and sixth centuries BCE onward, Greek mercenaries and merchants are historically and archaeologically visible in Egypt, and Naukratis became a major Greek trading settlement. [A: archaeology/texts] Real contact provided a plausible environment for hybrid stories and competing claims. But contact is evidence for possible transmission, not proof of the origin of this particular tale. [D]

Near Eastern comparisons: marriage, kingship, and broken obligations

The Hebrew Bible’s story of David and Bathsheba in 2 Samuel links royal desire for another man’s wife to violence and political disaster. [A] Yet its sequence and theological purpose differ sharply from the Helen tradition, and there is no evidence of direct literary transmission. Mesopotamian royal and epic texts likewise show marriage as an instrument of dynastic order, but no secure Near Eastern prototype can be identified for the specific pattern “foreign prince takes queen, coalition launches war.” [D]

Hittite diplomatic texts offer a more historically grounded comparison. In Late Bronze Age international politics, dynastic marriages, sworn treaties, fugitives, extradition, and breaches of obligation could all become matters between kingdoms. [A] That context shows that a royal marriage triggering interstate conflict was not an alien political idea in the world behind later Greek epic. It does not show that a specific Hittite treaty was the source of the Oath of Tyndareus. [D]

Ancient art does not issue a single verdict either

The visual record is equally revealing. A Corinthian black-figure column-krater of about 590–570 BCE in the Metropolitan Museum of Art (27.116) shows Paris and Helen standing together in a chariot, surrounded by Trojan couples; the museum describes the subject as the marriage of Paris and Helen. [A: artifact] This differs markedly from the modern default image of a screaming woman dragged aboard a ship. Other fifth- and fourth-century images emphasize leading away, persuasion through Aphrodite and Peitho, or Menelaus’ later recovery of Helen. [A: artifacts]

This episode’s representative image is that early Corinthian krater, c. 590–570 BCE. The ancient object belongs to the Metropolitan Museum of Art, and the Wikimedia Commons file derives from the Met’s Open Access material. It matters because it prevents us from projecting one later visual formula backward onto all Greek antiquity. Like the texts, the images preserve competing ways of telling the event.

So who is responsible?

Center Paris and the story becomes one of violated hospitality and taking another man’s wife. Center Helen and desire and choice come forward. Center Aphrodite and divine manipulation of human longing becomes decisive. Go back to Zeus’ war plan in the lost Cypria, and even the lovers’ choices sit inside a larger divine design. [A/B/C] Reach Euripides’ Helen, and the supposed object of the entire war is a phantom.

These contradictions should not be forcibly harmonized. [D] Myth is not a police dossier but a tradition repeatedly rewritten by communities, poets, historians, and performers. Helen can be a regretful runaway, a human being overpowered by a goddess, a blamed woman, or an innocent double-victim of reputation and war. That instability is one reason she remained one of antiquity’s most powerful figures.

Now the oath begins to operate

When Menelaus returns, Helen and property are gone and Paris’ ship is already eastward. The oath once sworn in Tyndareus’ house becomes a demand for action. Kings and heroes are summoned one by one. Not all of them want to go. [C]

Later tradition makes the irony especially sharp for Odysseus, the very man credited with devising the oath. He pretends madness and ploughs a field until Palamedes exposes the ruse by placing the infant Telemachus in the plough’s path. [C] Achilles is still young, and Thetis knows that Troy offers him immortal fame at the price of a short life. She therefore hides him at the court of Skyros, in traditions that will soon bring Odysseus to his door. [C]

A promise designed to preserve peace begins pulling Odysseus from Ithaca, while a mother’s attempt to save her son cannot keep Achilles out of the coming war. The ships have not sailed yet, but Greece is already being emptied of its heroes.
