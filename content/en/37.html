EPISODE 37 · ILIAD BOOKS 11–17

The Man in Achilles' Armor — Patroclus and the Burning Ships

Patroclus · Achilles' armor · Sarpedon · Apollo · Euphorbus · Hector · layered causation

The embassy of Episode 36 fails to move Achilles, but the battle does not wait for negotiation to succeed. Major Achaean fighters are wounded, the Trojans cross the defenses, and the fighting reaches the ships. [A] Patroclus goes out to learn what is happening, sees the wounded, and returns in tears. When Achilles asks what has happened, the center of the poem shifts. The question is no longer simply whether Achilles will return, but how long the army can survive without him.

Patroclus asks permission to lead the Myrmidons wearing Achilles' armor, hoping the Trojans will mistake him for Achilles and withdraw from the ships. [A] The problems of recognition and misread signs from Episodes 19 and 21 have become military strategy. At battlefield distance, identity can be reproduced materially before a face is seen. Achilles agrees but gives a limit: save the ships, then return; do not pursue all the way to Troy's walls. [A]

The deception works. The Trojans believe Achilles has returned and recoil. [A] Patroclus saves the ships and drives the counterattack forward. At that point the plan has succeeded. Yet success blurs the boundary Achilles set. Patroclus continues. To reduce his death to “he disobeyed an order” would erase the divine interventions and layered battlefield causation that follow. [D]

At the center of the fighting stands Sarpedon, son of Zeus and leader of the Lycians. Zeus considers rescuing his doomed son, but Hera warns that other gods would then claim the same right for their children. Sarpedon dies at Patroclus' hands; Zeus nevertheless ensures that Apollo cleans the body and that Sleep and Death carry it home to Lycia. [A] A son of Zeus can still die in war. The mortal-divine boundary explored through Prometheus and Diomedes now opens in the opposite direction: divine kinship does not automatically cancel mortality.

Loose comparison is possible with semi-divine heroes and divine dynastic conflict in Mesopotamian and Hurro-Hittite traditions, but no evidence identifies a direct Near Eastern prototype for Sarpedon's death. [D] Homeric interest lies in holding Zeus' attachment and the limits of what he will change in the same scene.

Patroclus presses on and attacks the Trojan wall three times. On the fourth, Apollo drives him back. [A] Soon Apollo strikes from behind and disorients him, his armor is stripped away, Euphorbus wounds him, and Hector delivers the final blow. [A] Dying, Patroclus himself orders the causes: Fate and Apollo first, Euphorbus among men, Hector third. [A] Few moments in the Iliad more clearly resist the urge to name one cause.

Hector strips Achilles' armor from Patroclus and puts it on himself. [A] Identity shifts again. The armor Patroclus wore so that others would see Achilles now moves onto Hector's body. Hector, who removed his helmet to hold his child in Episode 35, now wears the most famous armor of his enemy. The object has not finished changing meaning.

Briseis is not yet central to the battle, but in Iliad 19 she will mourn over Patroclus' corpse and say that he had comforted her with the possibility of marriage to Achilles. [A] His death therefore destroys more than Achilles' friendship. For a woman moved through war as a captive and prize, Patroclus had represented another possible future. The voice recovered for Briseis in Episode 30 becomes harder to ignore here.

The struggle for Patroclus' body continues. Achilles, still without armor, eventually appears at the edge of the battlefield and drives the Trojans back with his presence and shout before he has formally returned to combat. [A] This time there is no mistaken substitute. After the false Achilles dies, the real Achilles begins to come back.

The news reaches him. Achilles throws dust over his head and collapses in grief; Thetis hears his cry from the sea and rises to him. [A] In Episode 36 he could still speak of two possible fates. Patroclus' death changes the conditions under which those options mean anything. A long homecoming can no longer be the same life he imagined the night before.

Reference & Evidence Notes

[A] Iliad 16.844–857. Patroclus explicitly layers Fate, Apollo, Euphorbus, and Hector in the causation of his death.

[A] Iliad 19. Briseis mourns Patroclus and describes her own losses and his promised comfort.
