EPISODE 31 · ILIAD II

Why Is the Same Criticism Heroic from One Man and Beaten Out of Another?

Thersites, Odysseus, and the False Dream — War, Rank, and the Right to Speak in the Iliad

In the previous episode Achilles withdrew after Agamemnon seized Briseis, and Thetis asked Zeus to let the Achaeans suffer until her son’s honor was restored. Zeus nodded. His first weapon, however, is neither thunder nor a Trojan charge. It is a lie. In Iliad Book 2 Zeus sends Agamemnon a destructive Dream telling him that the hour has come to take Troy. Agamemnon treats the divine message as strategic intelligence; the audience already knows it is a trap. [A] At the very moment the supreme commander makes his most consequential judgment, his information source is corrupted. [D]

Agamemnon then devises an equally dangerous test. He tells the army the opposite of what he wants: let us abandon the war and sail home. [A] He seems to expect the warriors to resist the proposal out of honor. Instead the host surges toward the ships, raising dust and dragging vessels toward the sea. Men who have endured nine years of war are ready to end it at a sentence. [A] The scene exposes the Achaean force not as a modern national army with a single institutional will, but as a precarious coalition of contingents enduring a prolonged expedition. [D]

Hera intervenes through Athena, who finds Odysseus. Carrying Agamemnon’s scepter, he reverses the rout—but not with one style of persuasion. To kings and prominent men he speaks respectfully and explains that Agamemnon was testing the army. Ordinary soldiers he strikes with the scepter and orders to sit down and obey their betters. [A] The same panic receives different treatment according to rank. [D]

When the assembly settles, one man keeps talking: Thersites. Homer first marks him by a hostile portrait—bow-legged, lame, uneven-shouldered, with a pointed head and sparse hair—and says that he habitually abused Achilles and Odysseus. [A] The narrative ridicules his body before presenting his politics, revealing a heroic value system in which physical form, status, and authority can be made to reinforce one another. [D]

Yet Thersites’ argument creates the real difficulty. He asks Agamemnon what more he can want when his huts are already full of bronze and captive women. He accuses the king of taking first choice from spoils won by other men and explicitly recalls the seizure of Briseis from Achilles. [A] The substance overlaps strikingly with Achilles’ complaint in Book 1: Agamemnon is greedy, appropriates rewards produced by others’ fighting, and damages the honor system that holds the coalition together. [A/D]

The decisive difference is not simply what is said but who has the standing to say it. Achilles is the son of a king, the greatest fighter, and commander of his own Myrmidons. When he reaches for a sword against Agamemnon, Athena herself restrains him. Thersites receives no heroic genealogy, independent contingent, or idealized body. Odysseus does not refute him in debate. He strikes him across the back with the scepter. A welt rises, Thersites weeps, and the assembled army laughs. [A]

It is therefore too simple to read the episode merely as a wise Odysseus silencing a wicked troublemaker. It is equally anachronistic to turn Thersites into a modern democratic revolutionary. [D] The Iliad does not presuppose political equality. Councils of kings and assemblies of the wider host coexist, but prestige and speech are unequally distributed. The scene remains disturbing precisely because Thersites is not punished for saying something wholly false. He is a man without recognized heroic standing who says things that are, in important respects, true. [D]

The scepter sharpens the point. In Book 2 Homer gives Agamemnon’s scepter a genealogy: Hephaestus made it for Zeus; it passed through Hermes, Pelops, Atreus, Thyestes, and finally Agamemnon. [A] It is not a random stick but a portable sign of legitimate authority. When Odysseus uses it to control the army, symbolic kingship becomes physical coercion. [D] The same object that authorizes elite speech lands on the back of a common soldier.

A comparison with Mesopotamia is revealing if kept limited. At the beginning of the Epic of Gilgamesh, the people of Uruk suffer under the king’s excessive exercise of power and their complaint reaches the gods, who create Enkidu as a counterforce. [A] There is no evidence that the Thersites episode was borrowed from this story. [D] The contrast is more useful than a claim of dependence: in Gilgamesh, communal suffering reaches the divine sphere and changes the plot; in Iliad 2, criticism from a socially marginal warrior is closed down by force and laughter.

Hittite treaties and oath texts come from a different genre, yet they repeatedly formalize hierarchical loyalty, obligation, and military-political dependence. [A] They cannot be equated with Homer’s assembly. [D] Even if the identification of Ahhiyawa with an Achaean-related world is broadly persuasive, no Hittite tablet gives us a historical ‘Thersites.’ What the comparison can show is that status, oath, and loyalty were powerful political languages in the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean.

The Hebrew Bible offers another instructive contrast. Nathan confronts David over Uriah and Bathsheba, and Elijah confronts Ahab over Naboth. [A] But their right to challenge kings is grounded not in egalitarian citizenship but in prophetic authority: they speak as bearers of a divine word. Thersites possesses no comparable recognized warrant. [D] Across these traditions, criticism of rulers can exist, but its fate depends heavily on who is authorized to speak and from what source of authority.

Egyptian royal ideology typically presents the pharaoh as a maintainer of ma’at, the proper order linking gods, king, and society. [A] There is no close Egyptian mythic equivalent to Thersites openly attacking a commander in a mass military assembly. Hurrian succession myths are rich in conflicts over divine kingship but likewise provide no responsible one-to-one parallel. [D] Comparison is strongest when it marks limits rather than manufacturing similarities.

Archaeology also requires restraint. Linear B tablets from Mycenaean palatial archives attest a highly differentiated administrative world, including the wanax and numerous officials and occupational groups. [A: documentary/archaeological] They do not prove that the assembly in Iliad 2 is a transcript of a thirteenth-century BCE war council. [D] Homeric epic is better treated as a long traditional formation in which memories of the Late Bronze Age and later social experience could coexist.

Once Thersites has been beaten and the laughter subsides, the army becomes an army again. Odysseus speaks, Nestor organizes the host, and Homer moves into the famous Catalogue of Ships. [A] The catalogue can feel like a pause in the story, yet here it performs a crucial transformation: the men who moments ago were hauling ships toward home are reordered under regions, leaders, and contingents. [D]

The Trojan forces now move as well. But Homer does not immediately give us the expected collision of massed armies. Instead two men step into the space between them: Paris and Menelaus. The two figures most directly bound to the cause of the war are finally offered a chance to settle it in single combat, with Helen and the wealth at stake. [A]

If Menelaus wins, the war should end. In fact, he comes very close to killing Paris. Yet a goddess refuses to let the war finish. After Apollo’s plague and Zeus’ deceptive dream, the next episode asks an even sharper question: if human beings make peace and the gods break it, can responsibility for the Trojan War still belong to mortals alone?

Reference & Evidence Notes

[A] Homer, Iliad Book 2: Zeus’ deceptive Dream, Agamemnon’s test, the rush to the ships, Odysseus’ restoration of order, Thersites’ speech and beating, and the Catalogue of Ships.

[A] Linear B palace tablets attest hierarchical administrative titles and institutions but not the Thersites episode itself.

[A/D] Epic of Gilgamesh: Uruk’s complaints against Gilgamesh offer a controlled comparison in communal suffering under powerful leadership; no direct borrowing is claimed.

[A/D] Hittite treaties and oaths illuminate hierarchical loyalty in Late Bronze Age Anatolia; they do not prove identity with Homeric institutions.

[A/D] Hebrew Bible, 2 Samuel 12 and 1 Kings 21: Nathan and Elijah criticize kings through prophetic authority, contrasting with Thersites’ lack of recognized standing.
