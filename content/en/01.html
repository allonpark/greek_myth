EPISODE 01

Before the Gods — The Greek World Begins with Chaos

Chaos · Gaia · Tartarus · Eros · the birth of Uranus

When people think of Greek mythology, the first name that often comes to mind is Zeus: king of the gods, wielder of the thunderbolt. Then come Poseidon, who shakes the sea; Hades, ruler of the dead; Athena, goddess of wisdom and war; Apollo, god of prophecy and music. Their fame makes them feel primordial, as though they must have stood at the beginning of everything. But when we open Hesiod’s Theogony and begin from the first lines, the story starts in a very different place. There is no Zeus, no Olympus, and no divine kingdom in the form we later recognize. Instead there is a difficult word: Chaos. Hesiod begins with the stark declaration that “first Chaos came to be.” [A]

Modern readers hear “chaos” and think of disorder, confusion, or a system spinning out of control. Yet the Greek word khaos is not simply the modern idea of mess. Scholars have long connected it with a gap, opening, chasm, or yawning space. [D] Even that should not be forced into modern physics. Hesiod does not tell us that Chaos was empty space, a vacuum, energy, matter, or a scientific state before the Big Bang. The safest way to put it is this: Chaos is the first primordial condition or entity to appear before the cosmos has taken on the familiar structure of earth, sky, sea, and divine rule.

This immediately creates an important contrast with the opening of Genesis. In Genesis, God stands as the creator who precedes and orders the world. In Hesiod, no higher creator is said to make Chaos. Chaos appears, then other primordial beings emerge. Even the gods belong to the history of the cosmos. Zeus is born. His father Cronus is born. Before Cronus come Gaia and Uranus. In this sense the Theogony is not merely a catalogue of deities but a genealogy of power: a story explaining how the present order of the universe came to exist through generations of divine birth, conflict, succession, and rule. [A/D]

After Chaos came three crucial beings

After Chaos, Hesiod names three beings of enormous importance: Gaia, the broad-breasted Earth; Tartarus, the deep abyss beneath the world; and Eros. [A] The Eros who appears here should not simply be imagined as the later winged Cupid. Hesiod’s Eros is far more primordial. He is associated with desire, attraction, and the force that makes union and generation possible. [A/D] Before love becomes a human emotion in poetry, it appears here as a principle through which beings are drawn together and new life enters the cosmos.

There is another small but important distinction. Later summaries often say that Chaos “gave birth” to Gaia, Tartarus, and Eros. Hesiod does not actually say that. Chaos comes first; then Gaia, Tartarus, and Eros are named. Hesiod later gives Chaos descendants of its own, such as Erebus and Night, but Gaia is not simply presented as one of Chaos’s children. [A] Throughout this series, distinctions like this matter. Myth is most enjoyable when told clearly, but once later retellings and the earliest surviving texts are blended together, details invented centuries apart can start to look like a single original story.

Gaia was not merely a goddess of the earth — she was Earth

With Gaia, the cosmos gains something solid. Modern readers may instinctively picture her as a goddess who “rules over” the earth, but ancient myth does not always separate nature from deity so neatly. Gaia is a divine being and Earth itself. Uranus, who soon appears, is not simply the administrative god of the sky; he is the starry Sky. That matters because when Gaia and Uranus unite, the story is not only about the marriage of two gods. It is also a mythic way of imagining Earth and Sky joined together as the basic architecture of the world. [A/D]

Hesiod says that Gaia first produces starry Uranus, equal to herself, so that he may cover her on every side. She also brings forth the mountains and Pontus, the sea. [A] Zeus still does not exist. Poseidon does not yet rule the sea. Hades does not yet possess the underworld. Before the Olympians come vast divine realities that are almost indistinguishable from nature itself. The gods we know best are not the creators standing outside this universe. They are later generations who inherit, seize, divide, and stabilize a world that already exists.

At this point one of the central themes of Greek myth begins to emerge: power did not always belong to Zeus. Cosmic order is not made once and for all. Primordial beings appear, a younger generation is born, conflict follows, sons overthrow fathers, and still younger sons threaten to do the same. The sequence Uranus → Cronus → Zeus is therefore more than divine family drama. It becomes a mythic explanation for how the present order of the cosmos was established. [D]

Look east, and Greece no longer seems alone

Now cross the Aegean and look toward the ancient Near East. In the Babylonian creation epic Enūma Eliš, the beginning is associated with the mingling of Apsu, a divine freshwater principle, and Tiamat, linked with the salt sea. Generations of gods arise, conflict intensifies, and eventually the younger god Marduk defeats Tiamat and becomes the central divine ruler who orders the cosmos. [A] The details are very different from Hesiod, but one structural resemblance is worth noticing: the supreme ruling god is not simply supreme from the beginning; he comes from a later generation and gains his position through conflict with earlier powers. [D]

That does not prove that Greece merely copied Babylonia. The fact that Mesopotamian texts were written down earlier than Hesiod is important, but earlier surviving attestation is not the same thing as the moment a story was first invented. Greek traditions may have circulated orally for centuries before being written, and between Mesopotamia and Greece lay many interacting cultures: Anatolia, Syria, Cyprus, Crete, and the wider eastern Mediterranean. Bronze Age trade, diplomacy, migration, warfare, and craftsmanship created real routes of contact. Cultural transmission is therefore plausible, but in most cases we cannot identify the exact storyteller, port, court, or generation through which a particular motif moved. Such reconstructions belong to [D].

How is this different from the biblical beginning?

Set Hesiod beside Genesis and the contrast becomes even sharper. In Genesis, the creator is not one divine being generated within the world. God creates heaven, earth, light, waters, and living beings. In Hesiod, Earth and Sky themselves are divine beings, and their union produces further generations of gods. Biblical creation therefore strongly separates creator and created order, whereas early Greek cosmogony entwines nature, deity, genealogy, and political succession. [A/D]

Yet the comparison is valuable precisely because the traditions are not identical. Different ancient cultures looked at the same sky, land, sea, birth, death, storm, and darkness and asked related questions: What existed before the world took its present form? Why is the sky above us? Why does the earth support us? Why is there order rather than nothing? The answers differ. One tradition speaks of a creator. Another tells of generations of gods. Another begins from primordial waters. Comparative mythology becomes useful when it asks not merely “Are these stories similar?” but where the similarity stops and the difference begins.

Were humans absent from this early world?

A natural question now appears: while the gods are being born, where are humans? Modern retellings often arrange Greek mythology in a neat sequence: first the gods, then Zeus wins the cosmos, then Prometheus shapes humans from clay. This makes humanity seem much later than Zeus. The earliest surviving Greek material, however, is less tidy.

In Hesiod’s Works and Days, a Golden Race of humans already lives during the reign of Cronus. [A] Yet the Theogony gives no Genesis-like scene in which “the first human” is created at a single clearly defined moment. Later, when Prometheus disputes with Zeus over sacrifice, humans already exist. The famous story in which Prometheus literally shapes humanity from clay becomes clearer in later traditions, but it should not be projected backward as though Hesiod had told the same story. The question “When, exactly, were humans created in Greek myth?” has no single canonical answer.

This is a thread worth keeping. A later episode will return to the strange fact that some traditions place humanity before Zeus’s reign and imagine an earlier human age that was easier, richer, and less burdened by work and suffering. Why that world disappeared, and why humans came to labor, age, and fall ill, will lead us into Prometheus, Pandora, and the successive races of humanity.

Then Sky covered Earth

For now, return to Gaia. She has produced Uranus, the starry Sky who covers her, and their union begins to generate a new divine family. Oceanus, Hyperion, Iapetus, Rhea, Themis, Mnemosyne, and the youngest, Cronus, belong to the generation later called the Titans. After them come the Cyclopes, with a single eye, and the Hecatoncheires, beings with a hundred arms. [A]

Then something goes wrong. Uranus does not want his children to enter the light. Hesiod says that as they are born he hides them away in a secret place within Gaia. Earth keeps giving birth, yet the new generation is forced back into Earth. The cosmos is producing change while the ruler of the sky tries to prevent that change from appearing. [A]

Gaia suffers under the pressure and eventually devises a plan. She fashions a great sickle and asks her children which of them will act against their father. None answers easily. Their opponent is not merely a king. He is the Sky itself.

Then the youngest steps forward.

Cronus.

He tells his mother that he will do it. [A] What happens next is already violent enough to define the first great transfer of divine power. But the deeper surprise is that the story of a son attacking the genitals of an earlier sky god has a striking counterpart in much older Hurrian-Hittite traditions from Anatolia, where Kumarbi attacks Anu. Is this coincidence? A shared mythic inheritance? Cultural transmission across the eastern Mediterranean? The answer is not simple.

And an even larger question waits behind it. Cronus will overthrow a violent father and become king. Will he become a better ruler? Greek myth gives a memorable answer: the son who overthrows his father eventually learns to fear his own son.

The temptation is to turn “Chaos” into a finished philosophical system, but the surviving evidence does not require that neatness.
