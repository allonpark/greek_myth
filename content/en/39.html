EPISODE 39 · ILIAD BOOK 24

The Father Who Kissed His Son's Killer's Hand — Priam and Hector's Burial

Priam · Achilles · Hector's body · ransom · hospitality · burial · the ending of the Iliad

Even after Hector dies, his body does not return to Troy. Achilles drags the corpse around Patroclus' tomb, while the gods preserve Hector from physical destruction. [A] Divine dissatisfaction grows, and Zeus finally orders that Achilles accept ransom and return the body. [A] The last book of the Iliad turns away from the strongest fighter's victory and toward a more basic question: what may be done with the dead?

Priam makes an almost unimaginable decision. He loads treasure onto a wagon and goes personally into the Achaean camp. [A] He is not an official envoy protected by a network of peers; he is a father going to the man who killed his son. Hermes guides him safely to Achilles' hut. [A] In Episodes 34 and 36, inherited hospitality and elite standing provided a framework for negotiation. Priam has neither. He enters the enemy camp with the vulnerable status of a suppliant.

Priam kisses Achilles' hands and asks him to remember his father Peleus. He says that he has endured what no other man has endured: bringing to his lips the hand of the man who killed his son. [A] Achilles thinks of Peleus and weeps; Priam weeps for Hector. Their griefs are not identical. One has lost a son; the other is a son who will not return to his father. Yet for a time grief pushes the categories of enemy and ally behind the relations of father, son, old age, and death. [A/D]

Calling the scene simply “forgiveness” closes it too quickly. Priam never announces that he forgives Achilles, and Achilles does not confess regret for killing Hector. [A] What happens first is more concrete: the body is returned. The poem allows recognition of suffering without requiring a complete moral settlement.

Antigone's problem from Episode 14 returns here. She challenged a ruler's prohibition in order to bury her brother; Priam ransoms a son's body held by the enemy victor. The political settings and genres are different, but both keep open the question of whether the dead body can be fully absorbed into the power of the living. [A/D] Burial is not merely emotional closure; in Greek practice and literature it carries religious and social obligation.

Achilles has Hector's body washed and anointed away from Priam's sight, fearing that the father might explode in grief and provoke renewed violence. [A] Achilles himself helps place the body on the bier. Then he speaks to the dead Patroclus, explaining that he has returned Hector to his father and will give Patroclus a share of the ransom. [A] The death that produced Achilles' abuse of Hector also remains present when the abuse finally stops.

Priam and Achilles eat together. [A] The ransom looked like exchange; the meal creates a different temporary relation. In Episode 34, inherited guest-friendship stopped weapons between Diomedes and Glaucus. Here two men with no such prior bond share food only after the return of the body. It would be too simple to classify the scene as formal xenia, [D] but the suspension of hostility is enacted through concrete practices—gift, food, sleeping arrangements, safe passage.

Hebrew and Mesopotamian traditions offer many different forms of lament and concern for burial, but there is no evidence for a direct source behind Priam's meeting with Achilles. [D] Its strongest meaning is internal to the Iliad: violence is not ended, only interrupted long enough for mourning and burial.

Achilles guarantees Priam time for Hector's funeral. [A] In Troy, Andromache, Hecuba, and Helen lament Hector in turn. [A] The fear Andromache spoke in Episode 35 has become fact; Helen from Episode 26 remembers Hector as someone who treated her with humanity. [A] One death removes a different person from each mourner's world.

The Iliad does not end with the Wooden Horse, the flames of Troy, or Achilles' death. It ends with Hector's funeral. [A] The city has not yet fallen. From the next episode onward, no complete surviving Iliad can carry the narrative for us. Achilles' death, the Horse, and the sack must be reconstructed from summaries and fragments of lost epics, retrospective passages in the Odyssey, visual evidence, tragedy, and later Roman literature—and those layers must remain distinct. [B/C]

The funeral ends; the war does not. Troy's walls still stand, and Achilles still lives. From here the “end of Troy” survives not in one book but scattered across competing memories.

Reference & Evidence Notes

[A] Homer, Iliad 24. Direct source for Priam's journey, meeting with Achilles, ransom, shared weeping, meal, return of the body, and Hector's funeral.

[A] Iliad 24.486–595. Core sequence in which Priam invokes Peleus and Achilles returns the body.

[A/D] Burial comparison. Burial obligations are widely attested in Greek literature and material culture, while any direct literary genealogy between Antigone and Priam remains a separate question.

[A: artifact] British Museum E75. Attic red-figure cup, c. 480 BCE, by the Briseis Painter, showing Priam approaching Achilles' hut to ransom Hector; representative Commons photograph in the public domain.
