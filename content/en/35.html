EPISODE 35 · ILIAD BOOK 6

Knowing His City Would Fall — Hector and Andromache’s Last Quiet Moment

Iliad Book 6 · Hector · Andromache · Astyanax · honor and family · the fate of Troy

In the previous episode Diomedes and Glaucus lowered their spears in the middle of the battlefield because a bond of guest-friendship made by their grandfathers proved stronger, for one moment, than the war. Immediately afterward Book 6 of the Iliad turns inside Troy. Hector, following the seer Helenus’ advice, asks Hecuba to pray to Athena, urges Paris back toward battle, and then goes home looking for his wife Andromache and their little son. The house is empty. A servant tells him that Andromache, hearing that the Trojans were being driven back, ran to the wall. [A] Husband and wife therefore meet not in a bedroom or at a table but near the Scaean Gate, on the boundary between home and battlefield. Homer places the poem’s most private family conversation exactly where the city may be breached. [D]

Andromache takes Hector’s hand and weeps. His courage, she tells him, will destroy him. [A] Her fear is not abstract. War has already erased almost her entire family. Her father Eëtion ruled Thebe beneath Placus and was killed when Achilles sacked the city. Her seven brothers were killed by Achilles on a single day. Her mother was captured and ransomed, only later to die by Artemis’ arrows, according to Andromache’s account. [A] An earlier thread now returns: Achilles may be sitting out the present fighting, but the wider Trojan war has already reached families through his violence. To Andromache he is not merely a future danger. He is the man who destroyed her past.

That is why she tells Hector that he is now father, mother, brother, and husband to her. [A] The line is heavier than a declaration of love. Every kinship role that war has removed has been concentrated in one surviving person. If Hector dies, Andromache does not lose only a husband; she loses the last structure of family left to her. [D] Nor does she appeal only to emotion. She gives concrete defensive advice, telling Hector to station troops near the fig tree where the wall is easiest to assault. [A] Reading her merely as a passive grieving wife therefore narrows the text. She understands the cost of war and speaks about the practical defense of the city as well. [D]

Hector understands her. Then he makes an extraordinary admission: he knows in his heart that a day will come when sacred Ilios, Priam, and Priam’s people will perish. [A] This is not merely foreshadowing imposed by later readers who know Troy’s fate. Hector himself says it in the surviving text of Book 6. Yet what pains him most is not even the imagined deaths of his father, mother, or brothers. It is the prospect of Andromache being carried away captive to Argos, forced to weave for another woman and draw water under compulsion. [A] Beneath heroic language, the realities of defeat—captivity, enslavement, and the destruction of households—suddenly become visible. [A/D]

Hector imagines a future observer saying of the enslaved woman, ‘That was the wife of Hector, the greatest fighter among the Trojans.’ [A] The remark would renew her grief, and Hector says he would rather be covered by earth before hearing her cries as she is dragged away. Honor and love are therefore not simple opposites for Hector. He fights partly because he believes he must defend city and family, but the same honor system prevents him from remaining with them. [D] The tragedy is not that he leaves because he does not love them. It is that he loves them while believing that his identity and community require him to leave.

Why not remain inside the walls? Hector answers. He would feel unbearable shame before Trojan men and women if he withdrew like a coward; he has learned always to fight among the foremost and preserve the great glory of his father and himself. [A] The Greek concept of aidōs and the social character of heroic honor are relevant here. [D] Courage is not only an inward virtue. It is bound to the gaze of others, family reputation, and the public role of the warrior. The modern question—if he loves his family, why not simply stay?—is legitimate, but within Homer’s value-world staying could carry a form of social death of its own. [D]

Homer does not end this collision of values with an argument. Hector reaches toward his son, and the baby recoils into the nurse’s arms, frightened by the gleaming bronze and the horsehair crest nodding from his father’s helmet. Hector and Andromache both laugh. [A] One of the most famous domestic moments in the entire war is created by a child being afraid of the equipment of war. Hector removes the helmet and lays it on the ground, takes his son, kisses him, and prays to Zeus and the gods that the boy may become as great as his father—or greater—and that people may one day say he is far better than his father when he returns from battle. [A]

Read alone, the prayer is tender. Read within the larger Trojan tradition, it becomes cruelly ironic. Hector calls the boy Scamandrius; other Trojans call him Astyanax, ‘lord of the city,’ because Hector alone protects Ilios, Homer explains. [A] Yet Hector has just foreseen Troy’s destruction while praying that his child will someday rule it. Parents speak of a future even while knowing that the future may not exist. [D] This is one reason fate in the Iliad should not be reduced to mechanical determinism. People may know or sense death and still love, plan, and pray.

Hector gives the child back to Andromache, who smiles through tears. [A] He tells her not to mourn him before his appointed time, for no one can send him to Hades before fate, and no mortal can escape destiny once born. He then tells her to return to the loom, distaff, and management of the women, because war will be the concern of men. [A] The statement exposes the gendered division of labor and authority in Homeric society. [D] Yet the poem simultaneously gives one of its strongest accounts of what ‘men’s war’ does to women through Andromache’s own voice. The tension cannot be made neat.

The representative image for this episode is Anton Losenko’s 1773 painting Farewell of Hector and Andromache. It is not an ancient artifact but a later work of reception history [C], now in the Tretyakov Gallery. Wikimedia Commons marks the faithful reproduction of this two-dimensional public-domain artwork as Public Domain. It cannot be used as evidence for Bronze Age Trojan clothing, architecture, or the historicity of the scene. [D] Here it serves only to show how a later artistic culture imagined Homer’s farewell.

A comparison with Mesopotamian epic illuminates one aspect without establishing direct dependence. In the Epic of Gilgamesh, Gilgamesh frames the expedition against Humbaba through mortality and the pursuit of a lasting name: humans die, but fame may remain. [A] There is no demonstrated textual chain making Hector’s choice a borrowing from this episode. [D] The broad similarity is that mortal warriors answer unavoidable death partly through reputation; the important difference is that Hector’s dilemma combines reputation with the immediate survival of city, wife, and child. Cultural transmission can be discussed against the long history of eastern Mediterranean and Near Eastern contact, but direct borrowing of this scene is unproven. [D]

Hittite and Hurrian texts preserve worlds in which royal and military duty, dynastic survival, oath, and divine sanction are politically consequential. [A] Late Bronze Age Hittite documents mentioning Wilusa and Ahhiyawa independently attest a real zone of diplomatic and military contact between Anatolia and the Aegean. [A/D] They do not, however, permit us to identify Hector with a particular Hittite prince or turn his conversation with Andromache into a historical transcript. [D] Hurrian myths of divine kingship conflict likewise offer no direct counterpart to this family farewell. Contact environment and the historicity of an individual literary scene must remain separate questions.

Hebrew biblical war narratives repeatedly confront defeat, captivity, widows, and orphans. [A] That does not provide a direct parallel or source for Hector and Andromache. [D] The more defensible comparison concerns the structure of ancient warfare: defeat reshapes households, kinship, and the status of women and children, not merely the fortunes of kings. Egyptian New Kingdom battle reliefs and texts also display captives and war booty. [A: textual/iconographic] But Egyptian royal victory imagery speaks from the victor’s monumental viewpoint, whereas Andromache voices the fear of someone who may become the conquered. [D] The difference of genre and perspective is as important as any similarity.

Archaeologically, the Late Bronze Age fortifications, occupation levels, and destruction evidence at Hisarlık provide a material basis for studying a real defended settlement and episodes of destruction. [A: archaeology] The identification of Hittite Wilusa with Greek (W)Ilios is a strong but still scholarly historical-linguistic argument [D]; no destruction layer proves that Hector and Andromache spoke at a particular gate. Likewise, Linear B records from Mycenaean palaces illuminate palace economies and organized female textile labor [A: textual/archaeological], but they cannot be equated with Andromache’s individual predicted fate. Archaeology can establish a world in which walls, warfare, labor, and captivity were real. It cannot authenticate Homer’s private conversation. [D]

Hector finally puts the helmet back on. Andromache returns home, repeatedly looking backward and weeping. Once she arrives, the women of the household begin a lament for Hector even though he is still alive, because they do not believe he will return safely from battle. [A] It is a kind of anticipatory mourning. [D] Homer lets the audience experience the emotional consequences of Hector’s death long before narrating the death itself.

But death does not come yet. Paris arms himself and follows Hector out of the city. [A] The war accelerates again, and Achilles’ absence becomes increasingly expensive for the Achaeans. Eventually Agamemnon will decide to send gifts and an embassy to the man he dishonored. Odysseus, Ajax, and Phoenix will go to Achilles. [A] The next question, however, is not how large the compensation is. Agamemnon will offer Briseis back and promise enormous wealth, yet Achilles will initially refuse. [A] The problem of the ‘price of honor’ that began in Episode 30 returns: can an insult to honor simply be reversed by paying more property? The next episode enters Book 9 and Achilles’ stark choice between a short life with undying fame and a long life without it.

Reference & Evidence Notes

[A] Homer, Iliad 6.369–502. Primary surviving source for the meeting of Hector, Andromache, and Astyanax: the deaths of Andromache’s family, Hector’s anticipation of Troy’s fall, the frightened child, prayer, and farewell.

[A/D] Hittite texts and Wilusa/Ahhiyawa studies. Independent evidence for Late Bronze Age Anatolian-Aegean contacts. The Wilusa–Ilios and Ahhiyawa–Achaean identifications are strong scholarly arguments, not proof of individual Homeric characters.

[A] Egyptian New Kingdom war monuments and administrative texts. Comparative evidence for captivity, booty, and state representations of warfare, not a source for Homer’s scene.

[A/D] Hisarlık/Troy archaeology. Fortifications and destruction levels establish material contexts, not the historicity of Hector’s family conversation.

[C] Anton Losenko, Farewell of Hector and Andromache, 1773, Tretyakov Gallery. Later reception, not ancient evidence; representative image uses a Public Domain reproduction identified by Wikimedia Commons.
