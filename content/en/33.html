EPISODE 33 · ILIAD BOOK 5

The Day a Mortal Made the Gods Bleed — The Aristeia of Diomedes

Iliad Book 5 · Diomedes · Aphrodite · Ares · the boundary between gods and mortals

In the previous episode, peace collapsed with a single arrow. Menelaus had effectively won his duel with Paris and both armies had sworn an oath before the gods, yet Athena induced Pandarus to shoot and the war opened again. The Iliad now enters one of its strangest days. Achilles still refuses to fight, but another overwhelming Achaean warrior suddenly dominates the field: Diomedes, son of Tydeus. Homer briefly opens a boundary that should seem impassable. Diomedes does not merely defeat mortal warriors; he pierces Aphrodite’s wrist and eventually drives a spear into Ares himself. These central events survive directly in Iliad 5. [A]

This sequence is conventionally described as Diomedes’ aristeia, the sustained display in which one warrior achieves supreme battlefield excellence. [D] Yet his power is not simply physical. Athena makes fire blaze from his helmet and shield and grants him exceptional strength. [A] Mortal prowess and divine sponsorship operate together. Treating Homeric heroes as if they had fixed individual “power levels” misses the mechanism of the scene: who is strongest matters, but so does which god empowers whom, and when. [D]

Diomedes encounters Pandarus and Aeneas. Pandarus, the archer who broke the truce in the previous episode, wounds Diomedes but cannot stop him; Diomedes eventually kills him with a spear. [A] Homer does not explicitly label this divine punishment for the broken oath, so that conclusion would go beyond the text [D], but the narrative places Pandarus’ momentary prominence and death remarkably close together.

Diomedes then strikes Aeneas with a huge stone. As Aeneas falls, his mother Aphrodite enters the battle and wraps her arms and garment around her son. [A] A figure who will later become central to Roman foundation myth could have disappeared here. More important, however, is Athena’s prior instruction. She has cleared the mist from Diomedes’ eyes so he can distinguish gods from mortals and ordered him not to fight the immortals—except Aphrodite, should she enter the battle. [A] This is therefore not an autonomous mortal rebellion against heaven. It is a case in which one deity grants a mortal limited authorization to strike another deity. [A/D]

Diomedes pursues Aphrodite and pierces her wrist. Homer describes the goddess’s skin breaking and ichor, the fluid of the gods, flowing from the wound; the poet even explains that gods do not eat grain or drink wine as mortals do and therefore do not have mortal blood. [A] The paradox is striking: an immortal can be wounded, hurt, and cry out. Immortality is not the same as invulnerability. [D]

Apollo takes over the rescue of Aeneas while Iris removes the wounded Aphrodite from battle. On Olympus, Dione comforts her daughter by recalling precedents in which gods suffered: Ares was imprisoned by the Aloadae, and Heracles wounded Hera and Hades. [A] The idea that divine beings can suffer at the hands of extraordinary mortals or near-mortal opponents is therefore not unique to Diomedes. Yet the asymmetry remains: gods recover, while humans remain subject to death.

Zeus tells Aphrodite that warfare is not her province and that she should concern herself with marriage and love. [A] The passage exposes the gendered division of divine roles in Homeric ideology [D], but it does not make Aphrodite powerless. Only one book earlier she removed Paris from Menelaus and threatened Helen, altering the course of the war. Her power is not lesser; it is a different kind of power. [D]

On the battlefield Apollo carries Aeneas to his sanctuary at Pergamos, where Leto and Artemis heal him. Apollo creates an image of Aeneas to keep the fighting focused, while Ares intervenes for the Trojans. [A] Homer’s gods are not spectators. They evacuate casualties, create doubles, incite armies, deflect weapons, and fight. The battlefield is simultaneously a mortal and divine theater. [D]

Hera and Athena then descend. Athena mounts Diomedes’ chariot and now orders him to attack Ares, revising the earlier restriction against fighting gods. [A] When Ares casts his spear, Athena turns it aside; when Diomedes thrusts, she drives his spear into the war god. Ares roars like thousands of warriors and flees to Olympus. [A]

Reading this as a simple claim that “Diomedes is stronger than the god of war” loses the structure of the episode. [D] The conditions that make his spear effective are Athena’s command, his divinely granted sight, her defense, and her direct guidance of the weapon. Mortal force has not independently overthrown divine order; rather, a conflict among gods is being conducted through a mortal body. [D] Ares complains to Zeus, who rebukes him but still has him healed. [A]

A useful Mesopotamian comparison comes from the Epic of Gilgamesh. Gilgamesh and Enkidu kill the Bull of Heaven sent by Ishtar, and Enkidu insults the goddess; the gods subsequently determine that Enkidu must die. [A] There is no evidence that Homer’s scene of Diomedes wounding Aphrodite directly derives from this episode. [D] The broad similarity is heroic collision with a divine sphere; the crucial difference is that Diomedes acts under the authorization and active support of another goddess. Even where Ishtar and Aphrodite invite cultural-historical comparison, this particular combat scene should not be reduced to simple borrowing. [D]

Hurrian-Hittite traditions surrounding Kumarbi and Teshub likewise preserve violent struggles among divine beings for cosmic kingship. Divine conflict is directly attested in surviving textual traditions [A], but there is no close counterpart in which a mortal warrior successively wounds a love goddess and a war god. Cultural transmission across the interconnected Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean remains a legitimate field of inquiry [D], yet a claim of direct transmission requires intermediate evidence and a more distinctive bundle of shared motifs.

Genesis 32 offers only a limited Hebrew comparison: Jacob wrestles through the night with a superhuman/divine figure, receives a wound, a blessing, and a new name. [A/D] Jacob is not a battlefield hero driving a deity away; he himself leaves injured. There is no evidence for a common direct prototype. Egyptian traditions likewise preserve bodily violence among gods, notably in the conflicts of Horus and Seth [A], but no securely attested Egyptian narrative closely matches the sequence in which a mortal warrior wounds both a love goddess and a war god. [D]

Archaeology cannot demonstrate that a historical Diomedes wounded Ares. Late Bronze Age weapons, chariots, Mycenaean palace society, and the fortifications and destruction horizons at Hisarlık provide material contexts for studying the world from which epic memories may have developed [A: archaeology], but combat between mortals and gods belongs to a narrative and religious level. [D] Converting material similarities directly into proof of an individual Homeric hero’s historicity is a common category error.

The representative image for this episode is Gabriel-François Doyen’s Venus Wounded by Diomedes. It is not an ancient artifact but an eighteenth-century European reception of the Homeric episode. It is used here as evidence for later visualization of the myth, not as evidence for a Bronze Age event.

The point of Diomedes’ aristeia is not that mortals are stronger than gods. Even the most extreme moment in which a human makes gods bleed depends on divine permission and divine force. [D] Yet precisely because the boundary opens for a moment, Homer shows that it is not a simple wall. A mortal can see a god, ride beside a goddess, and wound an immortal. The mortal still dies; the god returns to Olympus and is healed.

In the next movement, Diomedes levels his spear at an enemy warrior and asks his lineage. Just before they fight, the two discover that their grandfathers once shared a bond of guest-friendship. They stop and exchange armor—bronze for gold. In the middle of a war, an inherited relationship older than the present commanders can prevent two enemies from killing each other. Their names are Diomedes and Glaucus. The next episode follows xenia, guest-friendship, and asks why its obligations could reach across generations and across a battlefield.
