EPISODE 06 · HUMANS AND FIRE

Who Made Humans? — Prometheus and Fire

The sacrifice at Mecone, the theft of fire, and conflicting traditions about human origins

After Typhon falls, Zeus’s kingship finally appears stable. The succession wars that carried the cosmos from Uranus to Cronus and from Cronus to Zeus have ended, and the story descends from heaven toward the human world. Until now the central question has been who rules the gods. From this point onward another question becomes decisive: what kind of relationship will exist between gods and mortals?

At the center stands Prometheus. In many modern retellings he is introduced as the Titan who molds the first human beings from clay. Yet the earliest surviving Hesiodic texts do not begin his story that way. When Prometheus becomes prominent at the sacrificial division at Mecone, humans already exist. In Works and Days, a Golden Race of humans even lives under Cronus, before Zeus becomes king. [A] To present clay-creation as though it were Hesiod’s single canonical account would mix later traditions back into earlier poetry.

Greek myth has no single “Adam moment”

Genesis gives a clear creation scene: humanity is formed from the ground and animated by divine breath. [A] There is a sharp before and after. Hesiod offers no equivalent universal moment in which the first human suddenly appears. Humanity is simply already present when key divine-human negotiations begin.

Later Greek and Roman traditions make the clay motif much clearer. Ovid, for example, preserves a version in which Prometheus forms human beings from earth mixed with water, shaped in the image of the gods. [C] That is an ancient tradition, but it belongs to a later literary layer than Hesiod.

Hesiod’s Prometheus does something different. Instead of creating human bodies, he changes the conditions under which humans live. He helps define sacrifice, obtains fire, and thereby alters the boundary between mortal and divine life.

The table at Mecone — the conflict begins with one ox

At Mecone, gods and humans divide the portions of sacrifice. Prometheus prepares an ox in two deceptive shares. In one portion he places edible meat and organs, covered by an unattractive stomach. In the other he places bones, wrapped in gleaming white fat. [A]

The second share looks better. Prometheus invites Zeus to choose. Zeus selects the fat-covered portion and the bones beneath are revealed. Hesiod’s wording leaves a complication: Zeus may already understand the trick. [A] The scene should therefore not be flattened into “clever Prometheus completely fools stupid Zeus.”

Scholars commonly read the episode as an aetiology for Greek sacrificial practice: bones and fat are burned for the gods while humans consume the meat. [D] Prometheus is not merely dividing an animal. He is helping establish a boundary between what belongs to gods and what belongs to mortals.

Zeus withholds fire

Angered by the deception, Zeus refuses fire to humans. [A] Fire is far more than light. It cooks food, hardens clay, works metal, protects against cold, and underlies countless technologies. To withhold fire can therefore be read as restricting the possibility of civilization itself. [D]

Prometheus acts again. He steals fire and carries it to humans hidden in a hollow fennel stalk. [A] Because such stalks can preserve a glowing ember, the detail has often been linked to real fire-carrying practice. [D]

Fire appears again in the human world. Zeus recognizes what has happened. This time he does not simply remove it again. Instead, he prepares a punishment that will enter human society in a far more enduring form.

Prometheus and Epimetheus — foresight and afterthought

Prometheus’s name is traditionally associated with “forethought,” while his brother Epimetheus is linked with “afterthought”—understanding only after the event. [A/D] The contrast becomes almost a narrative mechanism in the Pandora story.

Prometheus warns his brother not to accept any gift sent by Zeus. Epimetheus nevertheless receives the gift and only afterward remembers the warning. His name becomes his behavior.

The gift is Pandora.

Why did Mesopotamian gods create humans?

Now move east. In the Babylonian Atrahasis, the reason for human creation is remarkably practical. Lower-ranking gods are exhausted by digging canals and performing heavy labor. They rebel. The divine solution is to create a new being that will carry the work: humanity. [A] Clay is combined with a divine element, including the flesh and blood of a slain god.

The purpose of humanity is therefore to relieve divine labor. Hesiod does not offer the same systematic explanation. Genesis does not describe humans as a workforce created to release lesser gods from toil. Ancient cultures ask similar questions about human origins but answer them in very different ideological terms.

In Atrahasis, successful human multiplication eventually becomes a new problem. Humans grow numerous and noisy; plague, famine, and finally flood are used to reduce them. [A] That story will return when we compare Deucalion, Noah, and the Mesopotamian flood survivors.

Genesis also forms humanity from the ground

Genesis 2 describes God forming a human from the dust or soil of the ground and breathing life into him. [A] The linguistic relationship between Adam and adamah, ground or soil, is well known. Mesopotamia has clay plus divine substance; Genesis has soil plus divine breath; later Greek tradition has Prometheus and clay.

Yet the simple resemblance “humanity comes from earth” is not enough to prove direct borrowing. In agrarian societies, soil connects food, bodily mortality, burial, and the practical act of molding clay into human form. Similar imagery can arise independently. [D] Stronger arguments for transmission require more specific sequences of shared events. Flood traditions will provide a much more demanding test.

Egypt offered other human origins

Egypt never possessed a single unified creation theology. Heliopolitan, Memphite, Hermopolitan, and other traditions differed. In some Egyptian material, humanity is linked with the tears of the solar god Ra, supported in part by wordplay between terms for humans and tears. [A/D]

The ancient world cannot therefore be reduced to one formula in which “all peoples believed gods made humans from clay.” The more important comparison is what each culture believed humanity was for and how mortal life related to the divine order.

Humans keep fire, but receive a beautiful punishment

Prometheus has restored fire. Significantly, Zeus does not permanently remove it again. Humans retain the technological gift. Civilization moves forward. But the price arrives.

Zeus orders Hephaestus to mix earth and water and fashion a woman. Athena gives skills and clothing; Aphrodite gives beauty and desire; Hermes contributes deceptive speech and a cunning disposition. [A] Because the gods contribute gifts, she receives the name Pandora.

She looks like a gift.

In Hesiod’s logic, she is also a punishment sent to humanity. [A]

And with Pandora comes a vessel. Modern culture calls it a box. Hesiod’s Greek word is pithos—a large storage jar.

When its lid is opened, human life will never again be the same.

One thing remains inside at the end.

Elpis.

Usually translated as “hope.” But is it really the final consolation preserved for humanity, or something trapped away from humans inside the jar?

The next episode opens that jar.
