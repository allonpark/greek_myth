EPISODE 28 · THE FIRST DEATH AT TROY

Why Did the First Hero to Step onto Trojan Soil Become the First to Die?

Protesilaus and Laodamia — the glory of landing first, the war’s first casualty, and a return shorter than death

In the previous episode, the wind at Aulis began to blow again at the cost of a young woman’s fate. Whether Iphigenia actually died at the altar or Artemis carried her away to Tauris depends on the tradition, but the narrative fleet can finally move east. We naturally expect Achilles and Hector. Yet the first death at Troy belongs to neither. In Book 2 of the Iliad, the Catalogue of Ships remembers a less famous commander from Phylace: Protesilaus. Homer says that he was the first Achaean to leap from his ship and that a Trojan killed him. At home he left a wife tearing her cheeks in grief and a house still unfinished. [A] Homer makes the first death of the war memorable not through a vast battle scene, but through an unfinished home.

The familiar prophecy must be separated into evidence layers. The explicit formula “the first Greek to step on Trojan soil will be the first to die” does not appear in the relevant surviving Homeric passage. Homer directly attests the first leap and the death. [A] In the later Apollodoran tradition, Thetis warns Achilles that the first man to disembark will be the first killed; Protesilaus takes the risk and Hector kills him. [C] The ancient summary by Proclus of the lost Archaic epic Cypria likewise says that when the Greeks try to land at Ilion, the Trojans resist and Hector kills Protesilaus. Because the epic itself is lost, this is [B]. The conjunction of first landing and first death is therefore old, but the full prophecy should not simply be attributed to Homer.

The scene also makes practical sense as an image of amphibious danger. Soldiers climbing from ships cannot instantly form a stable battle line; carrying shields and spears through surf and sand leaves them exposed to defenders already established on land. [D] This does not turn the myth into a Bronze Age tactical report. It does, however, help explain why “who steps ashore first” could be imagined as a problem in which honor and mortality occupy the same place. The man in front can win the first glory, but he also meets the first spear.

In Proclus’ summary of the Cypria, after Protesilaus falls, Achilles kills Cycnus, son of Poseidon, and drives the Trojans back. [B] Pseudo-Apollodorus preserves a similar sequence: Hector kills Protesilaus; Achilles then lands with the Myrmidons, kills Cycnus, and the defenders retreat toward the city. [C] The distinction matters. Achilles is not the first sacrifice of the expedition. He enters only after another man has died, and immediately changes the balance of battle. The fear that drove Thetis to hide her son now begins to acquire military as well as maternal weight.

Yet Protesilaus survived in cultural memory less because he landed first than because of the person left behind. Homer does not name his wife, but deliberately tells us that she mourns at home. [A] Later tradition calls her Laodamia and transforms a battlefield notice into a supernatural marriage tragedy. In the Apollodoran Epitome, Laodamia continues to love her dead husband and makes an image of him. The gods pity her; Hermes brings Protesilaus up from Hades for a short reunion. She rejoices as if he had truly returned from Troy, but when he is taken back below, she kills herself. [C]

This later story recalls Orpheus and Eurydice while reversing the direction of travel. Orpheus is alive and descends to recover a dead spouse; in the Protesilaus tradition the dead husband is temporarily allowed upward into the world of the living. [C/D] Both stories imagine love pressing against the border of death, and both deny a permanent restoration. Greek myth permits exceptional crossings of Hades, but precisely because Heracles and a few others can cross, the ordinary human boundary becomes more visible.

Laodamia’s image of her husband opens another question: how can an object make an absent person present? Euripides wrote a Protesilaus, now lost, whose plot cannot be fully reconstructed; an ancient quotation preserves a line to the effect that a beloved one will not be abandoned even though lifeless. The lost-play evidence is therefore [B]. Later authors make the embrace of the husband’s likeness more explicit. [C] Modern scholarship can read the effigy not merely as a prop but as materialized mourning, an object occupying the gap between memory and body. [D] In a world without photographs, the likeness of a dead person could become an especially charged substitute for presence.

The representative image for this episode belongs to that later reception history. A Roman sarcophagus preserved at Santa Chiara in Naples depicts the myth of Protesilaus and Laodamia in a sequence of scenes and is generally dated to after the middle of the second century CE. [A: artifact] The Wikimedia Commons photograph by Sailko is reusable under CC BY 3.0. The sarcophagus is not evidence that the Trojan landing happened exactly as the myth says. It is direct evidence that, in Roman imperial funerary art, the story of Protesilaus and Laodamia remained a powerful language for death, separation, and conjugal attachment. [A/D]

A broad comparison with Mesopotamia brings to mind the Epic of Gilgamesh. After Enkidu dies, Gilgamesh cannot accept the loss; the death of his beloved companion forces him to confront his own mortality and drives his search for immortality. [A] Laodamia and Gilgamesh differ in relationship, gender, setting, and narrative purpose. There is no evidence that one story was copied from the other. [D] What can responsibly be compared is the human problem: the death of the loved person makes mortality impossible for the survivor to keep abstract.

Egyptian mortuary traditions move in another direction. The Osirian complex and funerary texts imagine the dead not as simply erased but as capable of continued existence within ritual and divine order. [A] Laodamia’s story instead concentrates on the inability of the living to accept absence. Hebrew biblical literature contains powerful scenes of mourning, but no close counterpart in which a dead husband is licensed by the gods to return to his wife for a few hours. [D] Here preserving the differences is more useful than forcing a common origin.

For Hittite and Hurrian comparison, the geopolitical background matters more than the temporary return from death. Hittite tablets from Hattusa preserve a treaty between Muwatalli II and Alaksandu, ruler of Wilusa. [A: text] The identification of Wilusa with Greek (W)Ilios and Ahhiyawa with an Achaean-related Aegean power has substantial scholarly support, but it remains an historical and linguistic argument and should be marked [D]. The resemblance between Alaksandu and Alexandros, another name for Paris, is striking, but it does not mean that a Hittite tablet has “found Paris.” Late Bronze Age western Anatolian politics and the heroic narrative shaped centuries later must remain distinct evidentiary layers.

Archaeology requires the same discipline. Hisarlık/Troy is not one city built once and destroyed once, but a sequence of settlements. Objects from Troy II that Schliemann associated with Priam belong roughly a millennium before the conventional horizon of the Trojan War, a chronological mismatch explicitly noted in museum discussions of comparable Early Bronze Age Anatolian material. [A: archaeology] Late Bronze Age Troy VI and VIIa are more relevant to debates over historical background, but no destruction layer identifies the spot where Hector supposedly killed Protesilaus. [D] Archaeology can recover cities, trade, destruction, and rebuilding; it does not automatically authenticate the actions of named mythical individuals.

Protesilaus nevertheless acquired cult and local memory in later Greek antiquity. [A/C] That does not prove a Bronze Age commander of this name existed, but it does show how communities could attach a mythic figure to places and rituals until he became part of lived social memory. [D] He was no longer merely the expendable first casualty. He became a hero of thresholds: ship and shore, marriage and war, life and death, departure and impossible return.

In that sense, the first death contains the whole war in miniature. A young man leaves home for honor. Marriage and domestic future remain unfinished behind him. His name survives because he dies, while the cost of that survival in memory is paid by the person left at home. [D] Achilles will later embody the same problem on a monumental scale when he confronts the choice between long life and undying fame. Protesilaus asks the question first and more quietly: can kleos, fame after death, compensate for the life that never gets built?

The Greeks do not sail home after burying their first casualty. Nor does the story jump immediately to year ten of the Iliad. The lost epic tradition preserves a long, shadowy prehistory of the siege. Achilles kills Cycnus, raids communities around Troy, and eventually waits for one of Priam’s young sons near the sanctuary of Apollo. The boy is Troilus. In later art his death becomes one of the darkest episodes attached to Achilles. Readers who know only the greatest version of the hero may find the next episode deliberately uncomfortable.
