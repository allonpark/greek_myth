EPISODE 40 · AFTER THE ILIAD

How Did Troy Fall After the Iliad Ended? — Achilles' Death, the Horse, and Competing Memories of the Sack

Aethiopis · Little Iliad · Iliou Persis · Achilles · Ajax · Neoptolemus · Wooden Horse · Priam · Cassandra · Andromache

The Iliad stops with Hector's funeral, but the ending most readers associate with the Trojan War lies beyond it. The difficulty is that no single surviving epic preserves that ending. Achilles' death is known largely through the ancient summary of the lost Aethiopis; the contest for his armor, Ajax's death, and preparations for the Horse through the Little Iliad; the sack itself through the Iliou Persis summary, retrospective passages in the Odyssey, visual evidence, tragedy, and later texts. [B/C] The method learned with Medea in Episode 18 becomes essential: familiarity does not create a single canonical version.

According to the ancient summary of the Aethiopis, Penthesilea and Memnon come to aid Troy and are killed by Achilles. Achilles himself is then killed near the city by Paris and Apollo. [B] The shadow of Apollo already lay near Achilles in the Troilus traditions of Episode 29, but the evidence does not justify turning Achilles' death into a simple punishment narrative for Troilus. [D] The surviving summary does not make that moral equation.

After Achilles dies, his armor becomes the object of competition between Odysseus and great Ajax. In the Little Iliad summary, Odysseus wins under Athena's influence and Ajax descends into madness and dies. [B] The armor that allowed Patroclus to become “Achilles” in Episode 37 continues to produce consequences after Achilles himself is gone, eventually passing to Neoptolemus. [B] Objects outlive owners and keep relationships in motion.

The conditions for Troy's fall also multiply: Helenus' prophecy, Philoctetes and Heracles' bow, Neoptolemus' arrival, the Palladium, and the building of the Horse appear across different layers of the tradition. [B/C] “Troy fell because of one wooden horse” is memorable, but the cyclic material imagines a cluster of requirements, prophecies, stratagems, and inherited obligations.

The Horse is already known inside the surviving Odyssey. Demodocus sings of the warriors hidden within it, while Helen and Menelaus recall the event. [A] More detailed Trojan debate—burn it, throw it from the cliff, or dedicate it to Athena—survives in the ancient summary of the Iliou Persis. [B] The Mykonos relief pithos, made around the seventh century BCE, gives very early visual evidence for the Horse and the sack motif. [A: artifact]

The Trojans bring the Horse inside and celebrate as though the war were over. At night the hidden warriors emerge and the returning Achaeans attack. [B] The broken truce of Episode 32 echoes faintly here: apparent endings repeatedly reverse into renewed violence. But the sack is not reducible to “Trojan stupidity.” The tradition layers deception, signal, prophecy, and religious judgment.

Priam dies at the altar of Zeus Herkeios at Neoptolemus' hands. [B] The old king who crossed the enemy camp in Episode 39 to retrieve his son's body is not protected even by the altar inside his own palace. Cassandra clings to Athena's image and is dragged away by Ajax the Lesser. [B/C] The violence of victory crosses military and sacred boundaries at once.

The fate of Andromache and Astyanax forces Episode 35 to be reread. The child once frightened by his father's helmet later becomes, in sack traditions, the child thrown from Troy's walls. [B/C: the responsible agent varies by tradition] Andromache becomes a captive. [B/C] No artificial omen was needed in the earlier family scene; later events are enough to change the emotional color of that earlier laughter.

Helen returns as well. Menelaus recovers her, but her responsibility, emotion, and degree of agency remain different across texts. [A/B/C] The question left open in Episode 26—abduction or choice—does not disappear because the war has acquired a victor.

One group, however, leaves the ruined city. Aeneas survives. In the Iliou Persis summary, after the omen involving Laocoön, Aeneas and his followers withdraw toward Ida. [B] Archaic Greek vase painting already shows Aeneas carrying his father Anchises from Troy. [A: artifact] Rome will later make this survivor central to its own origin story, but before that later re-foundation, the Greek victors still have to get home.

Troy has fallen. Yet a sack is only the last scene of a war, not the end of every life caught in it. Across the sea stand the homes the victors left years ago, and nothing guarantees those homes are waiting unchanged.

Reference & Evidence Notes

[B] Proclus, summaries of the Aethiopis, Little Iliad, and Iliou Persis. Ancient summaries of lost epics; principal evidence for Achilles' death, the arms contest, the Horse, and the sack.

[A] Homer, Odyssey 4, 8, 11. Surviving Homeric retrospective references to the Horse and sack.

[A: artifact] Mykonos Pithos 2240, c. 675–650 BCE. One of the earliest visual representations of the Trojan Horse and sack; representative photograph used under CC0 via Wikimedia Commons.

[B/C] Details concerning Priam, Cassandra, Astyanax, Helen, and the captives vary across cyclic summaries, tragedy, and later literature and are not harmonized into a single canonical account.
