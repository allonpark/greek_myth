EPISODE 30 · THE MOMENT THE ILIAD BEGINS

Why Did Achilles Stop Fighting? — Agamemnon, Briseis, and the Meaning of “Rage”

In the tenth year of the Trojan War, a priest’s plea becomes a plague, and the plague tears apart the supreme commander of the Achaeans and their greatest warrior. Iliad Book 1 is read here through honor, power, captivity, divine anger, and comparative traditions of kingship and epidemic disaster.

In the previous episode Achilles appeared in one of the darkest traditions attached to his name: the killing of young Troilus. Now the chronology leaps forward. Nine years of war have passed and Troy still stands. Homer does not begin the Iliad with Helen’s departure, the first landing, or the first clash beneath the walls. Its first word is mēnin: rage. The poet asks the goddess to sing the destructive rage of Achilles, son of Peleus, and immediately tells us that this rage brought countless sufferings upon the Achaeans. [A] The poem therefore opens not with Greeks fighting Trojans but with a fracture inside the Greek coalition itself.

The first agent of that fracture is neither Achilles nor Hector but an old priest. Chryses, priest of Apollo, comes to the Achaean camp carrying the god’s sacred emblems and a rich ransom for his daughter Chryseis. The army broadly favors accepting the ransom, but Agamemnon, who has received Chryseis as his war prize, insults and threatens the priest and drives him away. [A] Chryseis is not merely a romantic attachment. She is a captive woman distributed as booty. Agamemnon even imagines her growing old in his household. To soften this scene into a love triangle is to erase the coercive structure on which the quarrel rests. [D]

Chryses withdraws along the shore and prays to Apollo. The god hears. In the narrative world of the poem Apollo is not an abstract metaphor for disease but an acting deity with a bow. He descends in anger, strikes animals first and then men, and funeral fires burn continuously through the camp. On the tenth day Achilles calls an assembly. [A] The seer Calchas explains that Apollo is angry because Agamemnon dishonored his priest; Chryseis must be returned without ransom and propitiatory sacrifice must be made. We cannot treat this as an epidemiological case report, but the military reality behind the narrative is intelligible: epidemic disease in a crowded expeditionary force can destabilize command as effectively as an enemy army. [A/D]

Agamemnon agrees to surrender Chryseis, but only if he receives another prize. The Greek word geras matters here. It is not merely property; it is a publicly allocated token of status and recognized achievement. [A/D] Achilles argues that the booty has already been divided and promises compensation after Troy falls. Agamemnon refuses. If he must lose his prize, he threatens to take one from Achilles, Ajax, or Odysseus. At that instant the logic of the war turns inward: the issue is no longer Troy but hierarchy among the men besieging it.

Achilles explodes. He points out that the Trojans have never stolen his cattle or horses and have never attacked his distant homeland of Phthia. He came to fight for the honor of Menelaus and Agamemnon, yet he does the hardest fighting while Agamemnon takes the greater share. [A] This speech makes it difficult to imagine the Achaean host as a modern national army. Its leaders are elite warriors with their own contingents, interests, reputations, and claims to honor. When supreme command collides with the prestige of the strongest individual fighter, the coalition itself can fail. [D]

Agamemnon then makes the decision that drives the epic: he will return Chryseis but seize Briseis, the woman awarded to Achilles. [A] Popular retellings often frame this primarily as the theft of Achilles’ beloved. The political force of Iliad 1 is colder. Briseis is Achilles’ geras; taking her publicly declares that Agamemnon’s rank can override Achilles’ excellence. [D] Yet Briseis must not be reduced to a token in a male honor system. She is also a captive whose home and family were destroyed by war. In Iliad 19, when she finally speaks at length over the body of Patroclus, she remembers her dead husband and brothers and the destruction of her city. [A] The “prize” that measures heroic honor is a human being whose own catastrophe has already occurred.

Achilles considers drawing his sword and killing Agamemnon on the spot. Athena suddenly appears and catches him by the hair. She is visible to Achilles alone. Hera, who cares for both men, has sent her to restrain him. Athena orders him to use words, not the sword, and Achilles obeys. [A] Modern readers may interpret the scene psychologically as an image of self-restraint, but that is an interpretive layer [D]; within the poem’s own world Athena is an actual divine agent. Homeric causation repeatedly allows human emotion and divine intervention to operate simultaneously rather than forcing us to choose one or the other.

The sword returns to its sheath, but the verbal violence intensifies. Achilles calls Agamemnon greedy and cowardly and swears that a day will come when the Achaeans are dying before Hector and desperately need him. Nestor attempts mediation and fails. [A] Agamemnon’s heralds, Talthybius and Eurybates, eventually arrive at Achilles’ shelter. They stand in fear and silence. Achilles explicitly says the heralds are not to blame and orders Patroclus to bring Briseis out. She leaves with them unwillingly. [A] In that brief detail the poem preserves a fact the warriors’ negotiations continually obscure: Briseis’ will is not identical with the honor calculus of the men who exchange control over her.

The representative image for this episode is an ancient object closely tied to this scene: the Attic red-figure kylix British Museum E76 (1843,1103.92), attributed to the Briseis Painter and dated around 485–480 BCE. One exterior sequence shows heralds leading Briseis away while Achilles sits beneath his shelter, wrapped almost entirely in his cloak; the other side continues her movement toward Agamemnon. [A: artifact] The vase is not a photograph of a Bronze Age event. It is evidence that by the early Classical period the loss of Briseis and Achilles’ withdrawn grief had become a powerful visual memory of the epic. The representative photograph used here is traceable through Wikimedia Commons under CC BY-SA 4.0, with the object record cross-checkable against the British Museum.

After Briseis is taken, Achilles sits alone by the sea and weeps. He calls for his mother Thetis—the same sea goddess whose dangerous prophecy forced her marriage to the mortal Peleus earlier in this series. Achilles argues that if his life is destined to be short, Zeus should at least grant him honor; instead Agamemnon has humiliated him. [A] Thetis accepts his demand and approaches Zeus. At this point a personal quarrel becomes a strategic catastrophe. Achilles does not merely want an apology. He wants the Greeks to suffer until Agamemnon understands how much he needs him. Thetis asks Zeus to favor the Trojans, and Zeus finally nods assent. [A]

This is one of the Iliad’s most difficult ethical tensions. Achilles has genuinely been dishonored, and Agamemnon has abused command authority to confiscate an already allocated prize. Yet Achilles’ chosen remedy is to withdraw while his own comrades die. [A/D] Reducing the quarrel to “Achilles was right” or “Achilles was selfish” destroys the poem’s tension. Agamemnon’s coercive arrogance and Achilles’ destructive pride can both be true. The epic explores what happens when a social order built on competitive honor makes the status of elite men more important than the survival of the collective. [D]

A comparison with Hittite royal texts is illuminating if kept within strict limits. In the plague prayers of Mursili II, a devastating epidemic is connected with divine anger, inherited royal wrongdoing, and violated obligations. [A] The king investigates causes, acknowledges fault before the gods, and seeks ritual resolution. Iliad 1 likewise connects collective disease to a ruler’s religious offense and requires diagnosis, restitution, and sacrifice. But the genres are different: Mursili’s prayers are royal religious documents, while the Iliad is heroic epic. There is no evidence that a particular Hittite plague prayer served as Homer’s direct source. The defensible comparison is structural: ruler’s transgression → divine anger → communal disaster → investigation and ritual correction. [D]

Mesopotamian tradition offers another partial parallel. In Atrahasis, Enlil sends plague against humanity, and ritual action is used to respond to divine catastrophe. [A] Yet the cause is different: the gods object to humanity’s multiplication and noise, not to the insult of a priest. No textual chain demonstrates direct borrowing into Iliad 1. [D] The comparison instead shows that across ancient West Asia and the eastern Mediterranean epidemic disaster could be narrated not only as bodily disease but as evidence of broken relations between divine order and human society.

The Hebrew Bible provides a further independent comparison. In 2 Samuel 24, David’s census is followed by a plague that kills across Israel, linking a ruler’s act to communal death. [A] Again, this is not evidence that the Chryses episode derives from the biblical story. The theology and literary contexts differ. Egyptian royal ideology likewise made the king responsible for maintaining maat, the ordered relation between gods, society, and cosmos, but there is no close Egyptian counterpart to the precise Iliadic combination of captive women, competitive honor, and Apollo’s plague. [D] In comparative mythology, recognizing the absence of a close parallel is as important as finding one.

Archaeology also requires discipline. The Late Bronze Age Aegean and Anatolia certainly knew warfare, elite warrior cultures, captives, booty, palace authority, and international conflict. The identifications Ahhiyawa–Achaean and Wilusa–(W)Ilios have substantial scholarly support, though they remain historical-linguistic arguments rather than direct identifications of Homeric characters. [D] No destruction layer at Hisarlık and no Linear B tablet proves that Agamemnon seized Briseis and caused Achilles to withdraw. Homeric epic may preserve Bronze Age memories refracted through later Iron Age social values, but background plausibility is not event-level proof. [D]

We should therefore look at Briseis again. It is misleading to say simply that Agamemnon and Achilles “fight over a woman.” More precisely, they fight through control of her over their relative status. [D] Briseis is simultaneously a cause within the plot, a victim of conquest, and evidence of a war economy in which human bodies can become units of prestige. When she later speaks, the epic briefly allows the conquered woman’s memory to interrupt the heroic accounting of prizes and glory. That perspective will return with Andromache, Hecuba, and Cassandra, making it impossible to tell the Trojan War only as the story of heroic men.

Achilles’ rage already contains the seed of the next catastrophe. He withdraws, and the Myrmidons withdraw with him. Zeus, honoring his promise to Thetis, begins to permit Trojan success. Agamemnon still believes the army can win without Achilles. Soon the battle line will be driven toward the Greek ships. [A] And inside Achilles’ camp remains the companion closest to him.

Patroclus.

He is still alive. He has not yet worn Achilles’ armor or faced Hector. Yet the causal chain leading toward his death begins to move the moment Achilles leaves the battle. Without the seizure of Briseis the withdrawal would not take this form; without the withdrawal the Trojans’ advance toward the ships would unfold differently. At the same time, Zeus’ plan and the larger language of fate remain active in the poem. [A/D] This is why “rage” can stand as the Iliad’s first word: one man’s wounded honor changes deployments, divine decisions, thousands of lives, and ultimately the fate of the person he loves most.

But the next episode will not jump directly to Patroclus’ death. Agamemnon first believes he can win without Achilles. Zeus sends him a deceptive dream, and the entire Greek army nearly runs for home. In the resulting chaos a man stands up and hurls bitter accusations at the king. He is neither handsome nor royal; Homer calls him the ugliest man among the Achaeans.

His name is Thersites.

And the unsettling part is this: if we listen closely, many of Thersites’ accusations against Agamemnon sound remarkably like things Achilles has just said. Why, then, does the same criticism count as heroic rage when spoken by a prince, but become grounds for ridicule and violence when spoken by an ordinary soldier? The next episode enters Iliad Book 2 through a dangerous question: who is allowed to tell the truth?
